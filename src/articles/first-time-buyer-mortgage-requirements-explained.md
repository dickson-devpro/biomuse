**First-time buyer mortgage requirements in the US centre on four pillars: credit history, income documentation, down payment funds, and legal eligibility to borrow — and for foreign nationals, every one of those pillars has an extra layer of complexity.** The good news is that US mortgage programmes are genuinely accessible to immigrants, ITIN holders, and newcomers, provided you understand the rules before you apply. Read this guide in full before you speak to a single lender, and you will arrive at that conversation with a clear picture of exactly what is required — and what to fix first.

---

## Why This Matters in 2026

The US housing market in 2026 continues to present affordability challenges in most major metros, but mortgage access has broadened in meaningful ways for foreign-born buyers. Fannie Mae's updated foreign national underwriting guidelines, now in their post-2024 revised form, allow lenders more flexibility in accepting ITIN borrowers and non-traditional credit histories. At the same time, rates that rose sharply in 2023–24 have moderated into a range that makes long-term ownership more attractive compared with renting — particularly important for immigrants whose rent is not building equity or a US credit file.

For foreign nationals relocating for work, home ownership is also increasingly tied to longer-term residency planning. Understanding mortgage eligibility is therefore not just a housing question; it sits alongside the broader financial infrastructure you need to build in a new country. If you are still in the early stages of setting up that infrastructure — bank accounts, credit, cross-border transfers — our guide to [How Immigrants Can Buy a House in the US (2026 Guide)](/how-immigrants-can-buy-a-house-in-the-us/) covers the strategic overview, while this article drills into the specific loan requirements in detail.

## The Four Core Pillars of Mortgage Qualification

### 1. Credit History and Credit Score

Your credit score is the single most visible metric lenders use to price risk. In the US, scores are generated by the three major bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — using the FICO scoring model (and competing models such as VantageScore). Most mortgage underwriting uses a "middle score" drawn from all three.

**The practical score thresholds in 2026:**

| Loan Type | Minimum FICO | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| FHA (3.5% down) | 580 | Lender overlays often push effective minimum to 620 |
| FHA (10% down) | 500 | Very few lenders go this low in practice |
| Conventional (Fannie/Freddie) | 620 | Best pricing typically requires 740+ |
| VA (eligible veterans) | No official minimum | Most lenders require 620; some accept 580 |
| USDA | 640 (automated) | Manual underwriting available below this |
| ITIN loans (portfolio) | Varies by lender | Many require 660–700 or use non-traditional credit |
| Jumbo loans | Typically 700–720+ | Lender-specific; higher thresholds common |

**If you have no US credit history yet**, you are not automatically disqualified. Some lenders will perform manual underwriting — a human review of your file rather than an automated decision — using alternative credit references: rent payment history (with landlord letters or third-party rent reporting), utility accounts, insurance payments, and foreign bank statements. The process takes longer and requires more documentation, but it is a real pathway.

Building a US credit profile as quickly as possible should be a priority the moment you arrive. Our article on [How to Build a Credit File From Nothing in a New Country](/how-to-build-credit-file-from-nothing-new-country/) covers the fastest legitimate strategies, including secured cards, credit-builder loans, and becoming an authorised user on an existing account.

### 2. Income and Employment Documentation

Lenders want to establish that your income is stable, sufficient, and likely to continue. The documentation standard for a straightforward salaried borrower is:

- Two years of US federal tax returns (IRS Form 1040) or IRS transcripts
- W-2 forms for both years
- Pay stubs covering the most recent 30 days
- Employment verification letter confirming your role, salary, and length of service

**For immigrants with less than two years of US employment history**, lenders will often accept a combination of US records and foreign income documentation — particularly if your current US employer is the same company you worked for abroad. An offer letter plus a short US employment history can suffice in some cases, though you should expect more scrutiny.

**Self-employed borrowers** face a higher documentation bar:

- Two years of business and personal tax returns
- A year-to-date profit-and-loss statement (often required to be prepared by a CPA)
- Business bank statements for 12–24 months
- Evidence that the business is ongoing (active website, business licence, client contracts)

> **Illustrative example — salaried W-2 worker:** Amara relocated from Nigeria to Texas in early 2024 on an H-1B visa. By mid-2026 she has two full years of US tax returns, pay stubs from a technology employer showing $95,000 annual salary, and a solid employment letter. Her documentation package is clean and lenders can process it through automated underwriting. No foreign income documentation is needed.

> **Illustrative example — recent arrival:** Tomasz moved from Poland to Illinois in January 2025 on an L-1 visa. In mid-2026 he has only 18 months of US employment. His lender requests his Polish employment records (translated and notarised), a letter from his US employer confirming the internal transfer, and 12 months of US pay stubs. Some lenders decline; others approve with a larger down payment requirement.

### 3. Down Payment — Amount, Source, and Documentation

The minimum down payment varies by loan programme, but the source and documentation of those funds matter as much as the amount.

**Minimum down payment by programme (2026):**

| Programme | Minimum Down | PMI Required? | Key Eligibility Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fannie Mae HomeReady | 3% | Yes, until 20% equity | Income limits apply; first-time buyer education course required |
| Freddie Mac Home Possible | 3% | Yes | Area median income limits; at least one borrower must be first-time buyer |
| FHA | 3.5% (score 580+) | Yes (MIP for life of loan if <10% down) | Open to most lawful US residents including ITIN borrowers at some lenders |
| Conventional standard | 5–20% | Yes if <20% | Faster PMI removal than FHA |
| VA | 0% | No | Active duty, veterans, some surviving spouses only |
| USDA | 0% | Yes (annual fee) | Rural/suburban areas only; income limits apply |
| ITIN portfolio loans | 15–25% typically | Varies by lender | Not government-backed; lender holds loan |

**Sourcing funds from abroad** is common for immigrant buyers but requires careful documentation. Lenders will ask for:

- Bank statements from the foreign account for 60–90 days showing the funds were accumulated (not borrowed)
- Wire transfer records showing the funds moving to your US account
- An explanation letter if there are large or unusual deposits

Currency conversion costs can be significant on a large transfer. Before moving a down payment from overseas, compare your options carefully — our guide to the [Cheapest Ways to Move Money Between Countries 2026](/cheapest-ways-to-move-money-between-countries/) can help you avoid losing thousands to unnecessary fees and poor exchange rates.

**Gift funds** are permitted by most programmes, but must come with a gift letter signed by the donor stating no repayment is expected. Lenders will verify the gift was actually transferred into your account.

### 4. Legal Eligibility — SSN, ITIN, and Immigration Status

This is where foreign nationals face the most distinctive hurdles — and where understanding the landscape prevents nasty surprises.

**Permanent residents (green card holders)** are treated almost identically to US citizens by most lenders. You will need to provide your green card as a document, and if it expires within 12 months, be prepared for questions about renewal.

**Non-permanent residents on work visas** (H-1B, L-1, O-1, E-2, TN, and others) can access conventional and FHA loans at most mainstream lenders, subject to a key condition: your visa must have remaining validity, or you must demonstrate a history of visa renewals that makes continued US residency likely. Lenders are not permitted to predict visa renewal outcomes — that is above their remit — but they can and do consider the overall picture of your US presence. *This is not immigration legal advice; for questions about your specific visa status and what it means for a lender's risk assessment, consult a licensed immigration attorney.*

**ITIN borrowers** — typically people who pay US taxes but do not have an SSN — have access to a growing but still specialised set of lenders. These are mostly portfolio loans (held by the lender rather than sold to Fannie or Freddie) with requirements that vary significantly. Down payment minimums of 15–25% are common, rates run slightly higher, and documentation requirements are stringent. The trade-off is access to financing that would otherwise be unavailable.

**DACA recipients** occupy a complicated space. As of 2026, Fannie Mae guidelines permit DACA borrowers to access conventional financing, but FHA eligibility has varied by administration and lenders' own overlays. Always confirm current policy directly with any lender you approach.

## Debt-to-Income Ratio: The Hidden Qualifier

Your debt-to-income (DTI) ratio is the percentage of your gross monthly income absorbed by debt payments. Lenders calculate two figures:

- **Front-end DTI:** Your proposed housing payment (principal, interest, taxes, insurance, HOA fees) divided by gross monthly income
- **Back-end DTI:** All monthly debt obligations including housing, car loans, student loans, credit cards, and any other instalment or revolving debt, divided by gross monthly income

> **Illustrative worked example:** Priya earns $7,500 gross per month ($90,000/year). She has a $350/month car payment and minimum credit card payments of $100/month. She is considering a home with an estimated all-in monthly housing payment of $2,200.
>
> - Front-end DTI: $2,200 ÷ $7,500 = **29.3%** ✓ (under the typical 28–31% front-end guideline)
> - Back-end DTI: ($2,200 + $350 + $100) ÷ $7,500 = $2,650 ÷ $7,500 = **35.3%** ✓ (comfortably under 43%)
>
> Priya's DTI profile is strong for both conventional and FHA programmes. If she had an additional $400/month in student loan payments, her back-end DTI would rise to **40.7%** — still acceptable but worth watching. If she carried a $700/month loan on top of that, her back-end DTI would exceed 47%, which would close off most conventional programmes and require FHA or a lender willing to use an exception.

Paying down high-balance revolving debt before applying is one of the most direct ways to improve DTI and simultaneously boost your credit score by reducing credit utilisation.

## Common Mistakes First-Time Buyers Make — And How to Solve Them

1. **Applying before their credit file is ready**
   *The mistake:* Approaching lenders with a thin or non-existent US credit file and being surprised when every option requires a much larger down payment or is unavailable.
   *The solution:* Give yourself a minimum of 12 months — ideally 18–24 — to build at least three US trade lines before applying. Secured cards, credit-builder loans, and Experian Boost (for utility and streaming payment history) are your fastest tools. See our detailed guide on [How to Build a Credit File From Nothing in a New Country](/how-to-build-credit-file-from-nothing-new-country/).

2. **Moving down payment funds in ways lenders can't verify**
   *The mistake:* Bringing cash, using informal money transfer networks, or making multiple small transfers that obscure the source of funds — then being unable to provide a clean paper trail.
   *The solution:* Document every step. Use a regulated wire transfer or bank-to-bank transfer, keep every receipt, and leave funds in your US account for at least 60 days (90 is safer) before applying. Lenders call this "seasoning" the funds.

3. **Assuming all lenders have the same rules**
   *The mistake:* Getting one rejection from a major bank and concluding that a mortgage is impossible.
   *The solution:* Lender guidelines vary dramatically for non-citizen and ITIN borrowers. Community banks, credit unions, and specialist immigrant-lending institutions often have more flexible policies than national retail banks. Working with a mortgage broker who has specific experience with immigrant borrowers significantly broadens your options.

4. **Taking on new debt between pre-approval and closing**
   *The mistake:* Buying furniture on a store credit card or financing a car after receiving a pre-approval letter, then finding the loan falls through at closing because the new debt changed the DTI or credit score.
   *The solution:* Treat the period between pre-approval and closing as a financial freeze. Do not open new credit accounts, do not make large purchases on existing credit, and do not change jobs unless absolutely unavoidable.

5. **Underestimating total closing costs**
   *The mistake:* Saving exactly the minimum down payment and arriving at closing unable to cover the additional costs — typically 2–5% of the purchase price.
   *The solution:* Budget for closing costs separately from your down payment. They include lender origination fees, title insurance, appraisal, escrow setup, prepaid property taxes, and homeowners insurance. Some of these can be rolled into the loan or negotiated as seller concessions, but assume you need cash for at least 2–3% above your down payment.

6. **Ignoring mortgage insurance costs in their budget**
   *The mistake:* Focusing only on the advertised interest rate and ignoring the cost of private mortgage insurance (PMI) or FHA mortgage insurance premium (MIP), which can add $150–$400/month to a typical loan.
   *The solution:* Always model the all-in monthly payment including mortgage insurance when comparing programmes. FHA MIP is charged for the life of the loan if your down payment is under 10%; conventional PMI can typically be removed once you reach 20% equity, which may make a slightly higher conventional rate more cost-effective over the long term.

7. **Not getting a HUD-approved housing counsellor**
   *The mistake:* Skipping the free advice available from HUD-approved counsellors, particularly for navigating down payment assistance programmes that many immigrants are unaware they qualify for.
   *The solution:* The US Department of Housing and Urban Development maintains a searchable directory of approved counselling agencies offering free or low-cost advice. Many provide multilingual services. If you qualify for state Housing Finance Agency programmes, a counsellor will know which ones are open to your immigration status.

## After the Mortgage: Ongoing Costs to Factor In

Your mortgage payment is only one component of total homeownership cost. Budget separately for:

- **Property taxes:** Typically 0.5–2.5% of assessed value annually, depending on state and county. These are usually escrowed into your monthly payment.
- **Homeowners insurance:** Required by all lenders. Costs vary widely by location, construction type, and coverage level. Before you close, read our companion piece on [Property Insurance for First-Time Buyers Explained 2026](/property-insurance-first-time-buyers-explained/) — it covers what lenders require, what you actually need, and how to avoid being underinsured.
- **HOA fees:** If you are buying a condo or in a planned community, monthly HOA fees can range from $100 to over $1,000 and count toward your DTI calculation.
- **Maintenance reserve:** Financial planners commonly suggest budgeting 1% of the home's value per year for maintenance and repairs.

## Before You Apply: A Practical Pre-Application Checklist

Use this list to assess your readiness 3–6 months before you plan to apply:

- [ ] US credit score of at least 620 (580 for FHA minimum, but 640+ gives you more lender options)
- [ ] At least three active US trade lines reporting for 12+ months
- [ ] Two years of tax returns filed (US returns, or a combination with foreign documentation if newer arrival)
- [ ] Stable, documentable income for 12–24 months
- [ ] Down payment funds sitting in a US bank account for at least 60 days, with a clean source-of-funds trail
- [ ] Closing cost reserve (separate from down payment) of at least 2–3% of anticipated purchase price
- [ ] Valid immigration document with sufficient remaining validity (green card, visa, work authorisation)
- [ ] Back-end DTI calculated and confirmed below 43% with the target monthly payment included
- [ ] Pre-application meeting with a HUD-approved housing counsellor (especially if using down payment assistance)
- [ ] Mortgage broker or lender identified who explicitly confirms they work with borrowers in your immigration/documentation situation

If you are still in the early stages of building the financial infrastructure this checklist assumes — US bank account, initial credit, understanding of cross-border transfers — our guide to [Opening a Bank Account as a New Arrival in the US (2026)](/opening-bank-account-new-arrival-us/) is the right starting point, followed by the credit-building roadmap linked above.

## The Bigger Picture: Renting vs. Owning as an Immigrant

For some new arrivals, purchasing immediately is the right move. For others — particularly those whose US presence depends on visa renewals in the near term, or who are still deciding where to settle — renting for one to two years first makes both financial and logistical sense. Our [First-Time Renters Guide for New Arrivals in the US 2026](/first-time-renters-guide-new-arrivals-us/) covers how to navigate lease requirements, what landlords typically ask of foreign-national tenants, and how to use a rental period strategically to build the credit history you will need when you do apply for a mortgage.

The decision between renting and buying should also account for how long you plan to stay in a given location, the transaction costs of buying and selling (typically 8–10% of the home's value when you factor in agent commissions, closing costs, and moving expenses), and whether your income stability is sufficient to carry a mortgage through potential employment gaps.
