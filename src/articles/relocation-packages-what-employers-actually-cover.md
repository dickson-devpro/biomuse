**Most relocation packages look generous on paper but contain gaps that hit international hires hardest — from currency conversion losses to missing credit-history support. Understanding exactly what is and is not covered, before you sign, is the difference between a smooth landing and an expensive scramble.** Read this guide to decode every standard line item, identify what foreign nationals must negotiate separately, and walk into the offer conversation fully prepared.

---

## Why This Matters More in 2026

The US labour market for internationally mobile talent remains competitive across technology, healthcare, financial services, and specialist manufacturing. Employers who want the best candidates from a global pool have had to sharpen their relocation offers — but the structure of those offers has also grown more complex.

Three trends define the 2026 relocation landscape for foreign nationals specifically:

1. **Lump-sum packages have become the default.** Many mid-size employers have replaced managed relocation programmes with a single cash payment. That transfers cost risk directly to the employee. For a domestic hire with an established credit line, a local bank account, and an existing rental history, a $9,000 lump sum is workable. For a new arrival with none of those things, the same lump sum can disappear before the boxes are unpacked.

2. **Visa and immigration coordination is inconsistent.** Some employers treat visa sponsorship and relocation as entirely separate processes managed by different teams. The result is that employees sometimes land before their SSN is processed, before their payroll is set up correctly, or before anyone has confirmed whether the employer covers employee-side immigration legal fees. The administrative gaps cost real money.

3. **Tax treatment of relocation benefits has not simplified.** The suspension of most moving-expense deductions under federal law (introduced in 2017 and not restored as of 2026) means many reimbursements land on your W-2 as taxable income. International employees managing dual-country obligations face layered complexity — see our companion piece on [Tax Residency Explained When You Move Mid-Year (2026)](/tax-residency-explained-when-you-move-mid-year/) for how your tax residency status interacts with these payments.

## The Standard Relocation Package: A Line-by-Line Breakdown

### Moving and Shipping

The most visible relocation cost is physically moving your belongings. Employer policies vary substantially:

| Coverage Type | What It Typically Includes | What It Often Excludes |
|---|---|---|
| Full-service managed move | Door-to-door packing, shipping, unpacking, storage up to 30 days | High-value items (jewellery, art), vehicles, pets |
| Reimbursement model | You pay, employer refunds against receipts | Costs above a set cap; costs deemed non-essential |
| Lump sum only | Cash in hand; you organise everything | Nothing — all decisions and overruns are yours |
| Air freight allowance (international) | A fixed weight/volume of personal effects by air | Sea freight, vehicle shipping, excess baggage fees |

For international hires, the distinction between air freight and sea freight matters enormously. An air freight allowance of 100kg sounds reasonable until you calculate that a modest household fills a 20-foot container — roughly 1,100 kg of goods. If your employer covers only air freight, you are either shipping one suitcase's worth of personal items or paying sea freight yourself. That can run $3,000–$7,000 for a single-person move from Europe or Asia, more for a family.

**Illustrative example:** Aroha, a data engineer relocating from Auckland to Austin on an E-3 visa, received a package with a $10,000 lump sum. Her sea freight quote for a shared container came in at $4,200. After shipping, she had $5,800 left for temporary housing, security deposits, and setting up her apartment — far less than she had budgeted. Had she negotiated a separate shipping reimbursement capped at cost, the lump sum could have remained intact for settlement costs.

### Temporary Housing

Nearly all structured relocation packages include some form of temporary housing. The standard offering is 30 to 60 days in a hotel or serviced apartment, often booked through the employer's preferred vendor.

For foreign nationals, 30 days is frequently not enough. Finding a permanent rental without a US credit history is harder and slower than it is for domestic hires. Landlords may require larger security deposits, additional months of prepaid rent, or guarantors — processes that take time to arrange. Our [First-Time Renters Guide for New Arrivals in the US 2026](/first-time-renters-guide-new-arrivals-us/) covers exactly what landlords ask for and how to navigate each requirement.

**What to negotiate:** Ask for temporary housing to continue until your permanent lease begins, rather than for a fixed number of calendar days. Even if the employer declines, getting this in writing as a discussion item signals you understand the process — and occasionally HR will agree to a 15-day extension without pushback.

The type of temporary housing also matters. Serviced apartments are generally better value than hotels for stays over two weeks, and they allow you to cook, receive mail, and establish a sense of routine while you search for permanent accommodation. Our guide to [Short-Term Rentals vs Serviced Apartments on Arrival](/short-term-rentals-vs-serviced-apartments-on-arrival/) compares costs and practicalities in detail.

### Travel and Transportation

Standard packages cover:

- **One-way flights** for the employee (and family members, if applicable) in economy class; business class is negotiable for international moves over a certain distance
- **Car shipping or a vehicle allowance** — car shipping is expensive (often $1,500–$3,500 from coast to coast, more from overseas) and many employers offer a fixed allowance instead
- **House-hunting trip** — usually one round trip before the move, sometimes with accommodation for 3–5 nights, to allow the employee to view properties and confirm their choice of neighbourhood

What is rarely covered without asking: excess baggage fees on the relocation flight, pet transport (which can run $500–$2,000+ depending on destination and carrier), and travel for extended family members beyond a spouse and dependent children.

### Destination Services

Higher-tier packages — common in large corporations hiring internationally — include what the industry calls "destination services": a consultant who helps you navigate the practical realities of your new city. This can include:

- Area orientation tours
- School search assistance
- Lease negotiation support
- Bank account opening guidance
- Driver's licence and vehicle registration assistance

For foreign nationals, destination services can be genuinely valuable. Having a local expert who knows which landlords accept ITIN rather than SSN, or which banks are friendly to new arrivals without credit history, saves hours of frustrating discovery. If your package includes destination services, use every session.

---

### Visa and Immigration Fees

This is where many international hires encounter the most significant and least-discussed gap. There are two distinct buckets of immigration cost:

1. **Employer-side fees** — USCIS filing fees for petitions (e.g., H-1B, L-1, O-1), premium processing, and the employer's immigration attorney fees. Most sponsoring employers cover these as a matter of course, because they are legally the petitioner.

2. **Employee-side fees** — Consular visa application fees, SEVIS fees, document translation and notarisation, credential evaluation, and sometimes the employee's own legal representation. These are frequently not covered unless explicitly negotiated.

The total employee-side cost for an H-1B visa application — including the DS-160 fee, SEVIS fee, biometrics, and any document preparation — can reach $500–$1,500 depending on your home country and circumstances. For families, multiply accordingly. Ask HR, in writing, which fees are covered and which are not, before accepting the offer. If your employer does not address this, you can reference roles in comparable industries — for example, our overview of [Care & Support Roles That Sponsor Overseas Workers 2026](/care-support-roles-that-sponsor-overseas-workers/) shows how visa fee coverage is structured across different employer types.

### Miscellaneous and Settlement Costs

The final bucket is a long tail of smaller costs that individually seem manageable but collectively add up:

| Item | Typical Cost Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Security deposit on rental | 1–3 months' rent | Often higher for tenants without credit history |
| ITIN application (if no SSN yet) | $0 filing fee, but may need a certifying acceptance agent | Agent fees vary; budget $50–$200 |
| Credit-building products | Varies | Secured cards, credit-builder loans |
| Professional licence re-registration | $100–$800+ depending on profession | Nurses, engineers, architects may need state board fees |
| Credential evaluation | $150–$400 | Required by many professional licences and some employers |
| Household setup (linens, kitchenware) | $500–$2,000 | Not covered by most packages |
| Currency conversion losses | Variable | On sending money home or converting salary |

On currency conversion: if you are still servicing financial obligations in your home country — a mortgage, family support, a lease you have not yet exited — you will be converting USD to your home currency regularly. The rate matters. ## Full-Service vs Lump-Sum: A Realistic Comparison

| Factor | Full-Service Managed Package | Lump Sum |
|---|---|---|
| Who books and pays vendors | Employer's relocation management company | You |
| Cost risk | Employer absorbs overruns (within policy) | You absorb all overruns |
| Flexibility | Low — you use employer's vendors | High — you choose everything |
| Tax treatment | Often grossed up; employer handles complexity | Lump sum appears as income on W-2 |
| Best for | Families, international moves, first US relocation | Experienced movers, nearby domestic moves |
| Negotiability | Harder to change policy; easier to add items | Easy to negotiate upward; harder to add services |

**Illustrative example:** Tomás, a senior engineer relocating from Mexico City to Chicago on an L-1A visa, was offered a $15,000 lump sum. After sea freight ($5,500), 45 days in a serviced apartment ($5,400), one return flight for a house-hunting trip he paid himself ($620), and his family's visa application fees ($900), he had $2,580 remaining for security deposits, household setup, and building a US financial life from scratch. A full-service package with the same nominal value would have covered most of those costs through vendor relationships negotiated at volume rates — likely leaving him significantly better positioned.

## 5 Common Mistakes Foreign Nationals Make With Relocation Packages

1. **Accepting the first offer without asking questions.**
   *Solution:* Request the full relocation policy document, not just the summary in your offer letter. Policies contain caps, exclusions, and clawback terms that the verbal offer never mentions. Ask specifically: "Can you send me the written relocation policy that applies to my role and hire type?"

2. **Assuming temporary housing is sufficient to find permanent accommodation.**
   *Solution:* Start the rental search before you arrive if possible. Use the house-hunting trip (if offered) to view specific properties, not just neighbourhoods. If you have no US credit history, read [Renting With No US Credit History: What Landlords Accept](/renting-no-local-credit-history-what-landlords-accept/) before you negotiate with any landlord.

3. **Not building a US financial footprint before the relocation payment arrives.**
   *Solution:* Open a US bank account before you land if your visa status allows it — some banks and fintechs permit remote account opening. Without a US account, your first paycheck may be delayed, and you cannot receive a direct-deposit lump-sum. Our guide on [Opening a Bank Account as a New Arrival in the US (2026)](/opening-bank-account-new-arrival-us/) walks through which institutions accept ITIN and passport identification.

4. **Ignoring the clawback clause.**
   *Solution:* Read the exact language of the repayment provision. Confirm which events trigger it (voluntary resignation only, or also layoff?), whether the amount prorates by month, and whether the obligation survives if the company is acquired. If the terms seem unreasonable, negotiate them during the offer stage — this is normal and expected.

5. **Failing to account for the tax liability on the relocation payment.**
   *Solution:* If you receive a $10,000 lump sum and your marginal federal tax rate is 22%, approximately $2,200 of that is owed to the IRS. Some employers offer a "gross-up" — they pay additional funds to cover the tax liability. If no gross-up is offered, budget the net after-tax figure, not the headline number, when planning your move costs.

## What to Negotiate: A Practical Checklist

Before you respond to a relocation offer, run through this list and flag anything not explicitly addressed in the written policy:

- [ ] Is shipping covered separately from the lump sum, or included in it?
- [ ] What is the temporary housing duration, and is it extendable?
- [ ] Are visa application fees (employee-side) reimbursed?
- [ ] Are immigration legal fees for your personal representation covered?
- [ ] Is a house-hunting trip included, and does it cover a family member?
- [ ] Is a tax gross-up provided on cash relocation payments?
- [ ] What is the exact clawback trigger and proration schedule?
- [ ] Are spousal or partner career support services included?
- [ ] Is credential evaluation or professional licence re-registration reimbursed?
- [ ] Is there a cost-of-living differential or settling-in allowance?

Not every employer will say yes to every item — but asking costs nothing, and employers routinely add individual line items for candidates they want to hire.

## A Note on Employer-Sponsored vs Self-Funded International Moves

Not every international hire arrives with a corporate relocation package. If you secured a US role through a direct application, through a visa route that does not require employer sponsorship, or through a small business that has no relocation budget, you are funding the move yourself.

In that case, the same itemised framework above applies — you simply become your own relocation budget manager. The costs are the same; the risk and the planning responsibility fall entirely on you. Prioritise the items with the highest financial impact first: visa fees, temporary housing, and the financial products (bank accounts, secured credit cards) that unlock everything else. Locking in your first month's accommodation before you land — even at a premium — removes significant uncertainty from an already complex transition.
