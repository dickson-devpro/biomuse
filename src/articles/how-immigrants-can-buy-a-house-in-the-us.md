**Immigrants and foreign nationals can buy a house in the United States regardless of their citizenship status—there is no federal law restricting property ownership based on where you were born or what visa you hold. The real challenge is not legal eligibility but financing: without a US credit history or Social Security Number, conventional mortgage products are largely out of reach, and most newcomers need a deliberate strategy before they can close on a property. Read this guide before you start touring homes.**

## Why This Matters in 2026

The homeownership landscape for immigrants has shifted meaningfully over the past few years. A growing number of portfolio lenders and credit unions now actively market ITIN mortgage products, competing for a borrower segment that the big banks have historically underserved. At the same time, rising home prices in most major metros have made the larger down payments required by these lenders feel steeper than ever.

There is also a regulatory backdrop worth noting. Federal housing agencies have maintained—and in some cases clarified—eligibility rules for non-citizen borrowers across FHA, Fannie Mae, and Freddie Mac programs. If you hold a green card or a valid work-authorizing visa, you have more government-backed options in 2026 than you did a decade ago. If you are in the early stages of your US journey and still sorting out temporary accommodation, our [First-Time Renters Guide for New Arrivals in the US 2026](/first-time-renters-guide-new-arrivals-us/) covers the rental bridge you may need while you build the financial profile that lenders want to see.

Understanding where you sit in the immigrant-to-homeowner continuum is the first step. Let's break it down.

## Step 1: Know Your Legal and Financial Starting Point

### SSN Holders vs. ITIN Holders

Your most important distinction as a borrower is whether you have a Social Security Number or an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN), and whether you have started building a US credit file under either.

- **Social Security Number (SSN):** Issued to US citizens, green card holders, and most work-authorizing visa holders. If you have an SSN and have been responsibly using US credit products for 12+ months, your mortgage options are close to those available to any American.
- **ITIN:** Issued by the IRS for tax purposes to individuals who do not qualify for an SSN. You can apply on Form W-7. An ITIN alone does not create a credit score—you need to attach credit products (secured cards, credit-builder loans) to it. A growing set of lenders will underwrite mortgages using an ITIN plus alternative documentation.

### Immigration Status and Lender Eligibility

| Immigration Status | FHA Eligible? | Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac Eligible? | ITIN Loan Eligible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| US Citizen | Yes | Yes | N/A |
| Lawful Permanent Resident (Green Card) | Yes | Yes | Rarely needed |
| H-1B / L-1 / O-1 Visa Holder (valid) | Yes (case-by-case) | Yes (with conditions) | Yes |
| E-2 Treaty Investor Visa | Yes (case-by-case) | Limited | Yes |
| F-1 Student Visa | Rarely | Rarely | Yes |
| DACA Recipient | Limited (state-dependent) | Some lenders | Yes |
| No SSN / ITIN | No | No | Cash only |

> **Important:** Lender-level policies vary. The table above reflects general program eligibility, not a guarantee of approval from any specific institution. Always verify with a licensed mortgage professional.

### Building Your US Credit File From Zero

If you arrived recently and have no US credit history, expect to spend 12–24 months building a scoreable file before most mortgage lenders will consider you—unless you go the ITIN or foreign-national loan route, which uses alternative underwriting.

Practical steps to build credit faster:

1. **Apply for a secured credit card** tied to your SSN or ITIN. Use it for small, regular purchases and pay it in full monthly.
2. **Add a credit-builder loan** through a credit union. These typically report to all three bureaus.
3. **Ask to be added as an authorized user** on a spouse's or family member's established US credit card.
4. **Ensure on-time utility and rent payments are reported** through services like Experian RentBureau or similar platforms.
5. **Do not apply for multiple credit products simultaneously**—hard inquiries can suppress a thin file.

If you faced similar challenges when renting—as many immigrants do—you may have already encountered some of these workarounds. The article [Renting With No US Credit History: What Landlords Accept](/renting-no-local-credit-history-what-landlords-accept/) covers the rental-side version of this problem in detail.

## Step 2: Understand Your Mortgage Options

### Conventional Loans (Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac)

Fannie Mae's guidelines explicitly permit lending to non-citizen borrowers who have lawful residency and valid work authorization. Key requirements generally include:

- Minimum 620–660 credit score (lender-dependent)
- Debt-to-income (DTI) ratio typically below 45%
- Down payment of 3–20% depending on loan type
- Proof of lawful status (visa + I-94, green card, or Employment Authorization Document)
- Two-year work/income history (or less for some programs, with compensating factors)

Visa expiration is a nuance: lenders want confidence that your income will continue. If your visa expires in less than 12 months, some lenders will require evidence of renewal eligibility or employer sponsorship letters. Consult a mortgage broker who specialises in non-citizen borrowers.

### FHA Loans

Federal Housing Administration loans are government-backed and allow down payments as low as 3.5% (with a credit score of 580+). Lawful permanent residents qualify under the same terms as citizens. Non-immigrant visa holders may also qualify, but lenders implement these rules differently—some require additional documentation.

FHA loans carry mortgage insurance premiums (MIP) for the life of the loan in most cases, which adds cost. For buyers with limited down payment savings, however, FHA often represents the most accessible path.

### ITIN Mortgage Loans

ITIN loans are portfolio products—meaning the lender holds them on its own books rather than selling them to Fannie or Freddie. Because the lender absorbs the risk, the terms reflect that:

- **Down payment:** Typically 15–30%
- **Interest rate:** Usually 0.5–2 percentage points above conventional rates
- **Documentation:** 12–24 months of bank statements, ITIN tax returns (filed for at least 1–2 years), rental payment history, proof of employment or business income
- **Credit score:** Some lenders will work without a US FICO score; others set a minimum of 600–640

Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFIs), credit unions with immigrant-serving missions, and select regional banks are your best bets for ITIN products. Ask specifically whether the institution has an "ITIN mortgage program" or a "non-traditional credit underwriting" pathway.

### Foreign National Loans

If you live primarily outside the US and want to purchase investment property or a vacation home, foreign national loan programs exist separately from ITIN products. These typically require:

- 30–40% down payment
- Six to twelve months of cash reserves
- Proof of international income (employment letter, tax returns translated and notarised)
- No US credit history required (underwritten on assets and income alone)

Foreign national loans are not suitable for primary residence purchases by people who plan to live and work in the US full-time.

## Step 3: Assemble Your Documentation

Getting organised before you approach lenders saves weeks. The specific package depends on your status, but the following is a strong starting framework:

**For SSN borrowers with a US credit file:**
- Passport and visa / green card / EAD
- Last two years of US tax returns (or ITIN returns)
- Last 30 days of pay stubs; last two months of bank statements
- Gift letter (if any portion of down payment is gifted)
- Employer letter confirming position, salary, and visa sponsorship (if applicable)

**For ITIN borrowers:**
- ITIN letter from the IRS
- 12–24 months of personal bank statements
- 1–2 years of ITIN tax returns (even if filed voluntarily)
- Proof of on-time rent payments (cancelled checks or bank records)
- Reference letters from employer or accountant
- Explanatory letter about source of down payment funds

**For self-employed immigrants:**
- Two years of business and personal tax returns
- Business bank statements (12–24 months)
- CPA-prepared profit and loss statement
- Business license or registration documents

## Step 4: The Buying Process, Step by Step

### Get Pre-Qualified or Pre-Approved

Pre-approval carries more weight with sellers than pre-qualification. Approach two to three lenders simultaneously (mortgage rate shopping within a 14–45 day window counts as a single credit inquiry under FICO scoring rules).

### Work With an Experienced Real Estate Agent

Seek out an agent with specific experience serving immigrant or international clients. They will understand documentation quirks, be able to recommend immigrant-friendly lenders, and can help you navigate disclosures that assume US-citizen defaults.

### Make an Offer and Open Escrow

Once your offer is accepted, you will enter escrow—a neutral third-party process during which:
- The lender completes its full underwriting review
- A home inspection is conducted
- Title is searched and title insurance is issued
- An appraisal confirms the property value supports the loan amount

Escrow typically runs 30–45 days for conventional transactions; ITIN and foreign-national loans may take longer because of additional documentation review.

### Closing

At closing you will sign a large stack of documents and pay closing costs—typically 2–5% of the loan amount in addition to your down payment. These include lender fees, title insurance, prepaid taxes and insurance, and transfer taxes (which vary by state).

## Common Mistakes Immigrants Make When Buying a House in the US

1. **Assuming you cannot buy without a green card.**
   *Solution:* Many visa categories permit homeownership. Research your specific status and consult a mortgage professional before concluding you are ineligible.

2. **Applying for a mortgage before building any US credit history.**
   *Solution:* Give yourself 12–24 months of credit-building before approaching conventional lenders. ITIN loans are an alternative if you cannot wait, but they cost more.

3. **Choosing a conventional lender who has no experience with non-citizen borrowers.**
   *Solution:* Ask specifically how many non-citizen or ITIN mortgages the loan officer has closed in the past year. Inexperience leads to unnecessary denials and delays.

4. **Moving large amounts of money from abroad without documentation.**
   *Solution:* Lenders require a "paper trail" for down payment funds. If money is coming from overseas, document the source with bank statements, currency conversion records, and a gift letter if applicable. Bring funds into your US account at least 60–90 days before closing if possible.

5. **Ignoring FIRPTA implications before buying.**
   *Solution:* If you are not a US resident for tax purposes and plan to sell the property later, FIRPTA withholding (currently 15% of gross sale price) applies. Consult a CPA before you buy, not after you decide to sell.

6. **Underestimating total cash requirements.**
   *Solution:* Budget for down payment + closing costs (2–5%) + cash reserves (most lenders want 2–6 months of mortgage payments in the bank after closing). Many buyers deplete savings for the down payment and cannot cover closing costs.

7. **Conflating home-country investment strategies with US market norms.**
   *Solution:* In many countries, real estate transactions work very differently—timelines, legal structures, and financing norms vary widely. Work with professionals who understand both contexts. If you are currently evaluating options across multiple countries, resources like [How to Compare Residency Routes by Cost and Timeline](/how-to-compare-residency-routes-cost-timeline/) can help you think through the bigger picture of where to put down roots.

8. **Skipping title insurance.**
   *Solution:* Title insurance is standard in the US and protects you from undiscovered liens, ownership disputes, and errors in public records. It is not optional if you have a mortgage, and it is wise even for cash purchases.

## Cross-Border Considerations: Buying in the US While Evaluating Multiple Countries

Many of our readers are simultaneously weighing US homeownership against residency or property investment in Europe. This is a legitimate strategy—the US offers strong property rights and a liquid market, while European programs like those covered in [Golden Visa Routes Still Open in Europe 2026](/golden-visa-routes-still-open-europe-2026/) can tie real estate investment to residency rights in ways the US market does not.

A few practical points for anyone in this dual-track position:

- **Tax residency matters enormously.** Where you own property is not necessarily where you are tax-resident, and cross-border ownership creates reporting obligations on both sides. A CPA with international tax expertise is non-negotiable if you own real estate in more than one country.
- **Financing in one country does not transfer to another.** Your US mortgage and your European property loan are entirely separate credit relationships. The stronger your US credit profile, the better your US mortgage terms—independent of any foreign assets.
- **Estate and inheritance law differs dramatically.** If your long-term plan involves passing assets to family members in another country, the legal structure of ownership (individual, LLC, trust) matters. Get legal advice specific to your jurisdictions before closing.

## Working With the Right Team

Buying a home in the US as an immigrant is not a solo project. Your team should include:

- **A mortgage broker or loan officer** with documented experience in non-citizen or ITIN lending
- **A real estate agent** familiar with international clients
- **A real estate attorney** (required in some states; strongly recommended in all)
- **A CPA** with cross-border tax knowledge, especially if income comes from outside the US or you own foreign assets
- **An immigration attorney** if any aspect of your purchase or financing might interact with your visa status—this is a line this article will not cross, but it is a real consideration

The combination of a knowledgeable lender and a real estate attorney who reviews every document before signing is the single most effective risk-reduction strategy available to immigrant buyers.

## Final Thoughts

Buying a home in the United States as an immigrant is achievable—but it rewards preparation far more than spontaneity. The borrowers who navigate this process most smoothly are those who started building their US credit profile and documentation trail well before they felt ready to buy, worked with professionals who had done this before, and understood clearly that the financing challenge, not the legal right to own, was the hurdle to clear.

Whether you are eighteen months into your H-1B, filing taxes on an ITIN, or holding a green card and simply unsure where to start, the path exists. Map it carefully, build your team early, and let the numbers tell you when you are genuinely ready to close.
