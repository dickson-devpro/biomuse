**Before you reply "yes" to that US job offer, run the numbers — not just the salary figure, but the full financial picture including taxes, housing, cross-border costs, and the one-time expenses that hit foreign nationals disproportionately hard.** A $30,000 raise can evaporate entirely when you move from a low-tax Sun Belt city to a high-cost coastal metro, and the gap widens further if you're sending money home, rebuilding a credit profile from scratch, or covering visa-related costs your colleagues don't face. This guide gives you a practical, step-by-step framework for doing that comparison properly before you commit.

---

## Why This Matters More in 2026

The landscape for internationally mobile workers has shifted in ways that make a rigorous cost of living comparison more important than ever. Remote-work flexibility has normalised the idea of negotiating job location alongside salary, which means candidates now routinely receive competing offers from companies in Texas, California, New York, and Washington state — cities with radically different tax and housing environments. At the same time, rent levels in many US metros remain elevated, and the Federal Reserve's rate environment over 2024–2025 has kept mortgage costs high enough that renting is the default for most newly arrived foreign nationals.

For immigrants and foreign nationals specifically, several 2026 realities sharpen the stakes:

- **Visa-linked job lock.** On certain work visas, leaving an employer before a set tenure triggers serious immigration consequences. If the financial terms turn out to be wrong, you may be stuck absorbing them for years. Getting the analysis right upfront is far cheaper than fixing it later.
- **Credit-building lag.** Foreign nationals typically arrive with no US credit file, which means higher security deposits, limited access to standard credit cards, and in some cities, landlord requirements for additional guarantors. These costs are real and should be budgeted explicitly.
- **Currency volatility.** The USD has experienced notable swings against major currencies since 2023. If you're comparing a US salary against what you earn at home — or planning to remit regularly — a thoughtful currency stress-test is essential.
- **Expanded hiring across sectors.** Healthcare, technology, and [care and support roles that sponsor overseas workers](/care-support-roles-that-sponsor-overseas-workers/) have all seen growth in 2026, which means more foreign nationals than ever are weighing US offers across geographically diverse locations.

## Step 1: Convert the Gross Salary Into a Net Figure

The salary on an offer letter is always gross. For a foreign national, the gap between gross and net is typically wider than for a US-born peer at the same income level because you may be ineligible for certain deductions in your early years, and because state income tax dramatically shifts the math depending on where the role is based.

### Federal Tax Basics for Newcomers

Federal income tax brackets in 2026 apply to all workers with US-source income, whether or not you are a permanent resident or citizen. Your filing status (single, married filing jointly, etc.) and the number of allowable deductions determine your effective rate. As a rough planning figure for salary analysis, foreign nationals on H-1B or similar visas often find their effective federal rate sits between 22–28% at mid-to-upper income levels, after standard deductions.

Importantly, Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) taxes are withheld from most visa holders' paychecks, though treaty exemptions apply in some cases — this is a tax question for a qualified CPA, not a cost-of-living calculator.

### State Income Tax: The Variable That Changes Everything

| State | Top Marginal Income Tax Rate (2026) | Notes for Offer Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| California | ~13.3% | Applies to income above ~$1M; effective rate for $100K earner ~9–10% |
| New York | ~10.9% (+ NYC ~3.9%) | NYC residents face combined state+city rate exceeding 14% |
| Texas | 0% | No state income tax; often offset by higher property taxes |
| Washington | 0% | No broad income tax; capital gains tax on high earners applies |
| Florida | 0% | No state income tax |
| Illinois | 4.95% (flat) | Flat rate regardless of income |
| Massachusetts | 5% (flat, +4% surtax on income >$1M) | Generally lower than NY/CA for mid-range earners |

*These are indicative 2026 figures; consult a CPA for your personal liability.*

**Illustrative Example — State Tax Impact:**
Two software engineers each receive a $130,000 offer. Engineer A takes a role in Austin, Texas; Engineer B takes the same role in San Francisco, California.

- Engineer A (Texas): No state income tax. Federal effective rate ~22%. Approximate net after federal tax and FICA: ~$92,000.
- Engineer B (California): State effective rate ~9%. Federal effective rate ~22%. FICA at standard rates. Approximate net after all taxes: ~$79,000.

The difference is roughly $13,000 per year — before a single dollar of housing cost is compared. This is illustrative; actual figures depend on filing status, deductions, and other income.

## Step 2: Model the Housing Cost Realistically

Housing is typically the largest variable in any cost of living comparison, and foreign nationals face a structural disadvantage: without a US credit file, landlords often require higher security deposits, advance rent payments, or a co-signer. Budget for this upfront cash shock separately from ongoing monthly costs.

### Don't Forget the Upfront Cash Requirement

Beyond the security deposit, first-time renters in the US typically need to cover: first month's rent, last month's rent (in some markets), a broker's fee (up to one month's rent in NYC), moving costs, renters' insurance, and utility deposits if you have no account history with local utilities. In a high-cost market, this upfront cash outlay can reach $15,000–$20,000 before you even unpack.

## Step 4: Account for the Hidden Foreign-National Premium

Standard cost-of-living tools built for US residents miss a cluster of costs that are routine for foreign nationals. Quantify them explicitly.

**Money transfer costs.** If you remit $1,000 per month to family abroad, the difference between a bank wire (often 2–4% in fees and spread) and a specialist transfer service (often 0.3–1%) adds up to hundreds of dollars annually. Our guide to the [cheapest ways to move money between countries](/cheapest-ways-to-move-money-between-countries/) compares the main options available in 2026.

**Credit-profile building.** Without a US credit history, you pay more for security deposits and may be declined for financial products available to established residents. The investment required to build that profile — secured credit cards, credit-builder loans, and possibly a co-signed account — isn't enormous, but it should be factored in. See [How to Build a Credit File From Nothing in a New Country](/how-to-build-credit-file-from-nothing-new-country/) for a structured approach.

**Visa and immigration maintenance costs.** H-1B renewals, USCIS premium processing fees, and legal fees for extensions or employer changes are real line items. Annualise them and divide by 12 to include them in your monthly budget model.

**Tax filing complexity.** Foreign nationals often have more complex returns — potentially involving foreign income, treaty elections, and FBAR obligations. CPA fees for a complex non-resident return typically run $500–$2,000+ annually depending on your situation, compared with $100–$300 for a simple domestic return.

## Step 5: Stress-Test the Currency Dimension

If your financial life spans two or more currencies — which is true for most immigrant workers — your US salary's real value isn't fixed. It fluctuates with exchange rates.

**Illustrative Stress-Test:**
Priya earns $110,000 in Austin and remits $2,000 per month to her family in India. At a USD/INR rate of 84, she sends ₹168,000 per month. If the dollar weakens to 76 — roughly a 10% decline — the same $2,000 delivers only ₹152,000. That's a ₹16,000/month shortfall her family absorbs without any change to her US salary.

To protect against this, Priya could use a multi-currency account that allows her to hold USD and INR simultaneously and convert at favourable moments rather than on a fixed schedule. Tools and account types are covered in our comparison of [multi-currency accounts for expats](/multi-currency-accounts-compared-expats/).

## Worked Illustrative Example: Comparing Two Offers

**Scenario:** Marcos is a software engineer from Brazil. He receives two offers simultaneously.

- **Offer A:** $145,000 base salary at a company in San Francisco, California. No relocation package.
- **Offer B:** $118,000 base salary at a company in Austin, Texas. Includes a $10,000 relocation lump sum (taxable).

| Variable | Offer A — San Francisco | Offer B — Austin |
|---|---|---|
| Gross salary | $145,000 | $118,000 |
| Federal tax (est. effective rate) | ~24% | ~22% |
| State income tax (est. effective rate) | ~9% | 0% |
| FICA (Social Security + Medicare) | ~7.65% | ~7.65% |
| Estimated annual net salary | ~$86,000 | ~$83,000 |
| Monthly net salary | ~$7,167 | ~$6,917 |
| Monthly rent (1BR, city-centre) | ~$3,800 | ~$1,900 |
| Monthly net after rent | ~$3,367 | ~$5,017 |
| Relocation lump sum (net of tax, yr 1 only) | $0 | ~$7,500 |

**Bottom line (illustrative):** Despite a $27,000 lower gross salary, Offer B leaves Marcos with approximately $1,650 more per month after rent — roughly $19,800 more per year in disposable income. The San Francisco offer would need to be substantially higher to be financially equivalent.

This is an illustrative example using simplified tax assumptions. Marcos should consult a CPA before making a final decision, particularly around his visa type, filing status, and any Brazil-US treaty considerations.

## 5 Common Mistakes — and How to Avoid Them

1. **Comparing gross salaries without adjusting for state tax.**
   *Solution:* Always convert both offers to after-tax income first. Use a tax estimation tool or ask your employer's HR for a net pay illustration. Remember that state income tax is the single largest variable most candidates overlook.

2. **Using a national average rent figure instead of your actual target neighbourhood.**
   *Solution:* Research rents on active listings in the specific neighbourhood you'd realistically live in, given commute requirements and safety preferences. National or even city-wide averages often disguise enormous intra-city variation.

3. **Forgetting the upfront cash requirement.**
   *Solution:* Before you accept, calculate the total cash you'll need on day one: security deposits (potentially inflated due to lack of US credit history), first and last month's rent, moving costs, and temporary housing while you search. If that number exceeds your liquid savings, the timeline of the move needs adjustment.

4. **Treating the relocation package as pure income.**
   *Solution:* Read the clawback clause. If you leave within 12–24 months, you may owe the full package back. Also verify whether the package is treated as taxable income — it usually is — which reduces its net value. Our article on [relocation packages and what employers actually cover](/relocation-packages-what-employers-actually-cover/) breaks down the standard terms in detail.

5. **Ignoring the healthcare cost equation.**
   *Solution:* US employer health insurance plans vary enormously. An employer covering 100% of premiums with a low deductible adds meaningful value to your package; an employer offering a high-deductible plan with a large employee premium contribution could add $3,000–$8,000 per year in healthcare costs. Compare benefit costs alongside salary, not in a separate mental bucket.

## What About Long-Term Financial Planning in the New City?

A cost-of-living comparison isn't only about surviving the first year — it's also about whether the city gives you room to build wealth. Two additional dimensions are worth modelling.

### Can You Save Toward Homeownership?

In high-cost metros, the gap between renting and buying is extreme, and saving a mortgage deposit while paying $3,800/month in rent requires either a very high income or a very long timeline. In lower-cost metros, the math changes significantly. If homeownership is a medium-term goal, factor the realistic deposit-saving timeline into your offer comparison.

If you're curious about what buying looks like for foreign nationals in the US, [How Immigrants Can Buy a House in the US](/how-immigrants-can-buy-a-house-in-the-us/) is a useful companion read once you've settled on a location.

### Investment and Savings Capacity

Foreign nationals face some restrictions on which US investment accounts they can access, particularly as non-resident aliens. If building a US investment portfolio is part of your financial plan, understanding the available account types before you move is worthwhile. [Best Brokerage Accounts for Non-Resident Aliens 2026](/best-brokerage-accounts-non-resident-aliens/) outlines what's accessible depending on your residency and visa status.

## A Note on Tax Complexity When You Move Mid-Year

If you're relocating to the US partway through the calendar year, your tax filing situation for that year is more complex than most tools assume. You may have part-year residency status, foreign income from earlier in the year, and US-source income — all potentially on the same return. The financial impact of this transition-year complexity is worth understanding before you decide on a move date. Our overview of [tax residency when you move mid-year](/tax-residency-explained-when-you-move-mid-year/) explains the concepts in plain language; for your specific situation, a CPA with international tax experience is essential.

## Building Your Comparison: A Quick Checklist

Before accepting any offer, work through the following:

- [ ] Calculate estimated net salary after federal and state tax for each offer location
- [ ] Research actual current rents in the specific neighbourhoods you'd consider
- [ ] Quantify upfront cash needed: deposits, broker fees, moving costs, temporary housing
- [ ] Identify healthcare plan costs (employee premium + deductible + out-of-pocket maximum)
- [ ] Assess transport costs: car required or transit sufficient?
- [ ] Estimate annual visa/immigration maintenance costs (annualised to monthly)
- [ ] Include money transfer fees if you plan to remit regularly
- [ ] Factor in credit-building costs for year one
- [ ] Stress-test remittances at a 10–15% less favourable exchange rate
- [ ] Review the relocation package's clawback clause and tax treatment
- [ ] Model a rough deposit-saving timeline if homeownership is a goal within five years
- [ ] Confirm whether your spouse or partner can legally work in the proposed location

## The Bottom Line

A cost-of-living comparison done properly is not a five-minute exercise — but it's one of the highest-value analyses you'll ever run. The difference between a financially sound relocation and a financially stressful one often comes down to variables that never appear in the offer letter: state tax rates, credit-history penalties, currency costs, and healthcare exposure. For foreign nationals, those variables carry extra weight and extra complexity.

Run the numbers city by city, net of tax, with realistic housing costs and an honest accounting of the expenses that come with building a financial life in a new country. If the offer still looks strong after all that, it probably is. If it doesn't, you've just saved yourself from a very expensive mistake.
