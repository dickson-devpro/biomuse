Hiring from overseas is not a harder version of hiring locally. It is a different process with a different cost base, a licensing requirement, and a compliance regime that outlasts the hire by years.

Most employers approaching it for the first time budget for the visa fee, discover the skills charge, and then discover the licence. This guide sets out what the costs actually are, using the United Kingdom as the worked example because its figures are published clearly, and then sets out the principles that apply wherever you are hiring.

## You need a licence before you can hire anyone

In the UK, an employer cannot sponsor a skilled worker without first holding a sponsor licence. The licence is granted to the organisation, not to a role, and obtaining one involves demonstrating that you are a genuine operating business, that you have the HR systems to meet sponsor duties, and that the people named on the licence are suitable.

This matters for planning more than for budgeting. A licence application takes time. If you have found the ideal candidate and you do not yet have a licence, the candidate will usually be gone before you are ready. Employers who hire internationally with any regularity obtain the licence first and recruit second.

Licence fees vary by the size of the organisation and the routes applied for, and they are revised periodically, so check the current figure on the government's own fees table rather than relying on a number in any article.

## The Immigration Skills Charge

This is the one that surprises people, and it is substantial.

| Sponsor type | First 12 months | Each additional 6 months |
|---|---|---|
| Small or charitable sponsor | £480 | £240 |
| Medium or large sponsor | £1,320 | £660 |

Over a five-year sponsorship that totals **£2,400** for a small or charitable sponsor and **£6,600** for a medium or large one.

Four details that change the arithmetic:

**It is paid in full, up front.** Not spread over the period, not invoiced annually. When you assign the Certificate of Sponsorship, you pay the whole charge for the whole period at once.

**There is no six-month rate.** If the worker will be in the role for more than six months but less than a year, you pay for the full twelve months.

**Dependants are free.** The charge applies to the worker only. A sponsored worker bringing a partner and three children generates no additional skills charge.

**A job change costs only the extra time.** If you assign a new Certificate of Sponsorship to an existing worker, the charge applies to any additional period beyond the one already paid for, not to the whole new term.

## The exemptions worth checking before you pay

The Immigration Skills Charge does not apply in several situations, and some of them are easy to miss.

**Exempt occupation codes.** Codes 2111, 2112, 2113, 2114, 2115, 2119, 2161, 2162 and 2311 are exempt. These cover scientists, research and development managers, researchers and higher education teaching professionals. If you are a university, a research institute or an R&D-heavy business, check your codes before budgeting anything.

**Pre-April 2017 certificates.** If the worker was assigned a Certificate of Sponsorship before 6 April 2017, the charge may not apply.

**Study-to-work switchers.** No charge where the worker switches from a study visa to a Skilled Worker or Senior or Specialist Worker visa, and then extends that visa. Recruiting from the pool of international graduates already in the country is therefore cheaper than recruiting from overseas, which is a point worth making to a finance director.

**Certain Global Business Mobility transfers.** Where the certificate was assigned on or after 1 January 2023, the worker is an EU national or holds a Latvian non-citizen's passport, they normally work in the EU for your EU business, and the transfer is no longer than 36 months.

## Salary: the real cost driver

The skills charge is a fixed sum. Salary is where sponsorship actually gets expensive.

A sponsored skilled worker in the UK must be paid the higher of the general threshold, currently **£41,700 per year**, and the published going rate for their occupation code. A lower threshold of **£33,400** applies in certain cases outside healthcare and education.

Two consequences for employers:

You cannot sponsor someone into a role that pays below the threshold, even if that is the genuine market rate for the job in your region. The threshold is national.

And the going rate test means the occupation code you choose is a budget decision. If the going rate for the code is above the general threshold, that becomes your floor. Pick the code honestly — misclassifying a role to access a lower going rate is a compliance failure, and compliance failures cost licences.

## What the worker pays, and what you might absorb

The worker's own costs are considerable:

- Visa application fee, **£819** for up to three years from outside the UK, **£1,618** for more than three years
- Immigration Health Surcharge, **£1,035 per person per year**, paid up front for the full visa length
- Maintenance funds of **£1,270**, held for 28 consecutive days, unless you certify that you will cover the worker's costs for their first month

Nothing requires you to pay these. But a candidate with a family is facing a five-figure bill before they start, and for many roles that is the difference between accepting and declining. Employers who compete for international talent increasingly cover the visa fee and the health surcharge for the worker and dependants, and treat it as a relocation cost.

Note the distinction: the Immigration Skills Charge is an employer cost that cannot be passed to the worker. The visa fee and surcharge are worker costs that you may choose to absorb.

## The compliance obligations nobody budgets for

Holding a sponsor licence creates continuing duties, and they are the part that costs most in the long run because they consume staff time indefinitely.

You must keep specified records on every sponsored worker. You must report certain changes to the Home Office within set deadlines, including if the worker does not turn up, leaves, changes role significantly or changes work location. You must carry out and retain right-to-work checks correctly. You must keep the organisation's own details current on the sponsor management system.

Compliance visits happen, sometimes unannounced. The consequences of failure run from downgrading your licence to revoking it, and revocation cuts the visas of every sponsored worker you employ. For a business dependent on international hires, that is an existential risk sitting in an HR filing cabinet.

Budget for a named person who owns this, and for their training. "Someone in HR will pick it up" is how licences are lost.

## Outside the UK, the same shape

The specifics differ but the structure repeats across comparable systems.

**Australia.** Employers must be approved sponsors, nominate the specific position, and meet the Core Skills Income Threshold, which from 1 July 2026 is **AUD 79,423** for subclasses 482, 494 and 186, alongside the annual market salary rate for the role. Sponsors also contribute to the Skilling Australians Fund, and that levy may not be recovered from the worker.

**United States.** Employer-sponsored routes involve filing fees, often a labour condition or labour certification process, and attorney costs that typically exceed the filing fees. Certain costs are legally the employer's and cannot be passed to the worker. The H-1B route additionally runs on an annual cap with a registration process, meaning your ability to hire is constrained by a lottery rather than by budget.

The common principles: there is a licensing or approval step before you can hire at all; there is a wage floor you cannot negotiate below; some costs are legally yours; and the obligations continue for the life of the employment.

## Right to work checks, which are separate and mandatory

Sponsorship and right-to-work checking are two different obligations, and employers conflate them at their peril.

Every employer must check that every employee has the right to work, regardless of nationality, before employment begins. Doing this correctly gives you a statutory excuse against a civil penalty if it later turns out the person did not have permission. Doing it incorrectly, or not at all, leaves you exposed to penalties that are levied per worker.

The mechanics have moved steadily towards digital. Most people with time-limited permission now evidence it through an online share code rather than a physical document, and the check is completed through the government's online service. Manual document checks remain for some categories, and the rules about which applies to whom are specific.

Two points that catch employers out. First, the check must happen before the first day of work, not during onboarding week. Second, for people with time-limited permission, there is a follow-up check obligation when that permission is due to expire — and a calendar reminder is the whole system. Missing it is how compliant employers become non-compliant without noticing.

## The sponsor duties, in practice

A licence is not a one-off purchase. It creates obligations that run for as long as you sponsor anyone.

**Record keeping.** You must hold specified documents for each sponsored worker, including evidence of recruitment where required, contact details, absence records and a copy of their permission.

**Reporting.** Certain events must be reported through the sponsor management system within set deadlines, typically ten or twenty working days. These include a worker failing to start, leaving early, a significant change in role or duties, a change in work location, and changes to the sponsoring organisation itself such as a change of ownership or address.

**Monitoring.** You must be able to demonstrate that you know where your sponsored workers are and that they are doing the job described in the Certificate of Sponsorship.

**Cooperation.** You must allow access to the Home Office for compliance purposes, which can include unannounced visits.

The organisation also has to nominate people to key roles on the licence — an authorising officer, a key contact, at least one level one user — and those appointments carry personal suitability requirements.

## What a compliance visit involves

Visits can be announced or unannounced, and can happen before a licence is granted as well as afterwards.

An officer will typically want to see your HR systems, the files for sampled sponsored workers, evidence of right-to-work checks, and proof that you are monitoring attendance and reporting changes. They may interview sponsored workers, and they may interview their managers to establish whether the role being performed matches the role described.

The common findings are mundane: records held in several places and not reconciled, a reportable change that was noticed internally but never reported, right-to-work checks completed after the start date, a worker doing a materially different job from the one on the certificate.

The consequences run from a warning, through downgrading to a B-rating with a chargeable action plan, to suspension and revocation. Revocation curtails the permission of every sponsored worker you employ, which is why this should sit on an operational risk register and not only in an HR folder.

## The alternatives to sponsorship

Before committing to the cost and the compliance, check whether you need to sponsor at all.

**People who already have unrestricted permission.** Settled status, indefinite leave, dependants of other visa holders, graduates on post-study routes, and people on family routes may already be free to work for any employer. You conduct an ordinary right-to-work check and hire them. This pool is much larger than most employers assume and is routinely overlooked because candidates are screened out at the application stage by a box asking whether they need sponsorship — which many of them do not.

**International graduates already in the country.** Cheaper, because the study-to-work switch exempts you from the Immigration Skills Charge, and available immediately.

**Contracting through an established employer of record in the worker's own country.** Appropriate where the work genuinely can be done remotely and you do not need the person present. It is not a workaround for a role that requires presence, and it carries its own tax and permanent establishment questions.

**Restructuring the role.** Sometimes a role that cannot clear a salary threshold at the level advertised can be legitimately scoped at a higher level with broader responsibilities and a corresponding salary. This is a real option where the business need supports it. It is not an option as a paper exercise, and describing a role as more senior than it is on a certificate is precisely the kind of thing a compliance visit finds.

## A realistic five-year budget for one UK hire

For a medium or large sponsor, excluding salary:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Immigration Skills Charge, 5 years | £6,600 |
| Certificate of Sponsorship fee | per current fees table |
| Sponsor licence (apportioned) | per current fees table |
| Right-to-work and compliance admin | internal staff time |
| Optional: worker's visa fee absorbed | £1,618 |
| Optional: health surcharge absorbed, 5 years | £5,175 |

A medium or large employer who chooses to cover the worker's costs is looking at well over £13,000 across five years for a single hire, before any dependants and before salary.

## What this guide will not give you

A current sponsor licence fee, because it varies by organisation size and route and is revised periodically — take it from the government's fees table on the day you apply. An average processing time, because published service standards move. And any claim about how likely a licence application is to succeed, because no meaningful figure is published.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I pass the Immigration Skills Charge to the worker?**
No. It is an employer cost.

**Do I pay the skills charge for dependants?**
No, the charge applies to the worker only.

**Is the charge refundable if the worker leaves early?**
Partial refunds are available in defined circumstances, for example where the worker never takes up the role or where the visa is refused. Apply for it; it is not automatic.

**Do I need a licence to hire someone who already has the right to work?**
No. If the person already holds permission that allows them to work for any employer, you conduct an ordinary right-to-work check and hire them normally.

**What happens if my licence is revoked?**
Your sponsored workers' permission is curtailed. This is why compliance is an operational risk, not an administrative one.

**Is it cheaper to hire an international graduate already in the country?**
Often yes. The study-to-work switch exemption removes the skills charge, and the worker is already present.

**How long does a sponsor licence last?**
Licences are granted for a defined period and must be renewed. Diarise the renewal date, because letting a licence lapse affects every sponsored worker you employ.

**Can I sponsor someone for a part-time role?**
The salary thresholds are expressed as annual figures for the role, and part-time work makes clearing them difficult. The rules on how part-time salaries are assessed are specific, so take advice before advertising a sponsorable role at reduced hours.

**Do I have to advertise the job locally first?**
The resident labour market test was abolished for the Skilled Worker route, so there is no general requirement to advertise first. Other routes and other countries may differ, and genuineness of the vacancy is still assessed.

**What if the worker's role changes after they start?**
Significant changes to duties, salary or location are reportable, and some require a new Certificate of Sponsorship. A promotion is not an internal matter when the employee is sponsored.

**Can a small business get a licence?**
Yes. Small and charitable sponsors also pay the lower rate of Immigration Skills Charge. The obstacle is usually the compliance burden rather than eligibility.

**Is the Certificate of Sponsorship fee the same as the skills charge?**
No, they are separate charges. The certificate fee is per certificate; the skills charge is calculated on the length of sponsorship.
