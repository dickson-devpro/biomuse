**Europe's golden visa map has been redrawn. Several flagship programmes have closed or scaled back since 2022, but as of 2026 a meaningful number of routes remain open — and some have become more attractive precisely because their competitors have disappeared. If you are a non-EU national considering EU residency through investment, this guide maps the active options, their real costs, and the mistakes that derail applications.**

## Why This Matters in 2026

The golden visa landscape in Europe has never been more dynamic — or more confusing. Between 2022 and 2025, political pressure around housing affordability, EU-level scrutiny of third-country investor programmes, and post-pandemic policy reassessments prompted a wave of closures and revisions. Portugal removed residential real estate as a qualifying asset class. Spain announced a phased wind-down of its programme, citing housing market stress in cities like Barcelona and Madrid. The United Kingdom closed its Tier 1 Investor visa in 2022 with no direct replacement (see our coverage of [UK Investor Visa Alternatives After the Route Closed 2026](/uk-investor-visa-alternatives-after-route-closed/)).

For investors and their families, this compression of options has two effects. First, remaining programmes are absorbing greater demand, which is already pushing processing times outward in some jurisdictions. Second, the programmes still accepting applications have updated their terms — often raising minimum investment thresholds — making early, well-researched action more important than it has been at any point in the past decade.

For US-based foreign nationals in particular — those on H-1B, L-1, O-1, or other non-immigrant visas who may be uncertain about their long-term US immigration trajectory — a European golden visa can serve as a strategic insurance policy: a second residency that preserves optionality without requiring immediate relocation. That framing, however, comes with real financial and legal complexity that deserves careful attention.

## What "Golden Visa" Actually Means

The term "golden visa" is informal and covers a broad family of programmes. In practice, it refers to a residency permit (and in some cases a direct citizenship pathway) granted in exchange for a qualifying investment in the host country. The investment vehicle varies: real estate, government bonds, regulated investment funds, business creation, or direct capital transfer into a bank account. The residency permit is typically renewable as long as the qualifying investment is maintained.

Golden visas are **not** skilled-worker visas and do not require a job offer, employer sponsorship, or language proficiency in most cases. This makes them structurally different from routes like Germany's skilled-worker permanent residency (covered in our guide to [Germany Permanent Residency for Skilled Workers 2026](/germany-permanent-residency-requirements-skilled-workers/)) or the Netherlands' sponsored work routes. The trade-off is that they require substantial upfront capital.

### What Golden Visas Typically Provide

- The right to live and travel freely within the issuing country
- In most EU/Schengen member state programmes, visa-free travel across the Schengen Area
- The ability to include immediate family members (spouse, minor children, often dependent parents)
- A potential pathway to permanent residency and, eventually, citizenship — subject to residence and language requirements
- Continued maintenance of the qualifying investment for the permit's validity period

### Greece: The Reshaped Frontrunner

Greece remains one of the most-applied-to golden visa destinations in Europe, despite — or partly because of — the significant threshold increases rolled out in 2024. The two-tier system now applies: properties in high-demand zones (Attica, Thessaloniki, Mykonos, Santorini, and other island municipalities) require a minimum real estate investment of **€800,000**, while the rest of Greece retains a **€400,000** threshold.

Alternative non-real-estate routes exist, including investment in Greek government bonds and units of Greek investment funds, generally also at the €400,000 level. These routes have attracted attention from investors who are wary of the Greek property market's pricing or liquidity.

The Greek programme's headline advantage remains its **zero mandatory physical presence** requirement for maintaining the permit. You do not need to live in Greece to hold the visa — a feature that suits investors who want Schengen access as a backup rather than an immediate relocation.

**Illustrative example (Greece — real estate route):** An Indian national residing in the United States on an H-1B visa purchases an apartment in Athens for €850,000 (above the Attica threshold). After accounting for Greek transfer tax (currently 3.09%), notary and legal fees (~1–2%), and agent commission (~2%), the all-in acquisition cost is approximately **€910,000–€930,000** before annual property costs. She receives a five-year renewable residency permit covering herself, her spouse, and two minor children. No physical presence is required to renew. If she later decides to pursue Greek permanent residency, she would need to demonstrate five years of genuine residence in Greece. *This example is illustrative; actual costs depend on specific property, negotiation, and applicable taxes at time of purchase.*

### Malta: The Citizenship-Proximity Play

Malta's programme is distinct in that it is structured primarily as a **permanent residence programme** with a separate, accelerated pathway to naturalisation. The Malta Permanent Residence Programme (MPRP) requires a government contribution, plus either property purchase or a rental commitment, plus a further donation to a registered Maltese NGO.

The headline government contribution figure of €150,000 applies when purchasing property; if renting, the contribution rises to €98,000 for the administration fee structure (note: total outlay is broadly similar). Property purchase minimum is €375,000 (€300,000 in the South of Malta/Gozo). A €2,000 donation to a Maltese NGO is also required.

Malta's separate exceptional investor naturalisation route — which can lead to citizenship in approximately one to three years of genuine residence — has EU-level strings attached following European Commission scrutiny. As of 2026, the programme continues but is subject to a strict cap on annual naturalisations and genuine residence requirements. **Do not assume citizenship is automatic or fast; consult a licensed immigration attorney for current status.**

**Illustrative example (Malta):** A Nigerian entrepreneur based in Houston purchases a property in Valletta for €380,000 and pays the full government contribution plus fees. His all-in commitment including the property, government contribution, due diligence fees, legal fees, and NGO donation comes to approximately **€580,000–€620,000**. He receives Maltese permanent residence immediately for himself and his spouse. *This example is illustrative; costs vary by individual circumstances and applicable fees at the time of application.*

### Hungary: The Newest Entry

Hungary relaunched its golden visa programme in 2024 after a multi-year gap, this time centred on investment fund units rather than direct real estate. The minimum qualifying investment is **€250,000** into a government-approved real estate investment fund. A direct residential property route was also announced at a €500,000 threshold.

Hungary is an EU member state and full Schengen participant, which means permit holders gain Schengen Area travel rights. The programme is relatively new in its current form, so processing track records are limited. As with all programmes, professional due diligence on the qualifying fund vehicle is essential before investing.

### Italy: The Lifestyle Option

Italy's investor visa (Visto per Investitori) offers several qualifying routes at different investment levels: **€250,000** into an innovative startup, **€500,000** into an Italian limited company, **€1,000,000** for philanthropic donations, and **€2,000,000** into Italian government bonds. The startup route at €250,000 is the lowest headline entry point of any major Western European golden visa programme still active.

Italy does not require physical presence to hold the investor visa, but the path to Italian permanent residency and citizenship does involve residence requirements and Italian language testing. Italy's programme is less volume-driven than Greece's, which means processing can be smoother — though Italy's bureaucratic complexity in other areas is well-documented.

### Latvia: The Underappreciated Option

Latvia sits at the lower end of European golden visa minimums, with real estate investment routes from **€250,000** in Riga and **€50,000** in other regions (subject to eligibility conditions and company capital requirements). As a Schengen and EU member state, it offers the same core benefit of Schengen travel access.

Latvia is not as widely marketed as Greece or Malta, which means demand is lower and processing can be faster. For investors whose primary goal is Schengen access rather than a specific country's lifestyle or tax environment, Latvia deserves analysis. Use the structured comparison approach described in our guide to [How to Compare Residency Routes by Cost and Timeline](/how-to-compare-residency-routes-cost-timeline/) to model it fairly against higher-profile options.

## The Real Costs Beyond the Headline Investment

Every golden visa programme has a headline investment minimum, and that number is almost never the total cost. Investors who budget only to the minimum frequently face cash-flow surprises.

**Common additional cost layers:**

- **Government processing fees and due diligence charges:** Typically €5,000–€30,000 depending on programme and number of family members
- **Legal and immigration attorney fees:** €5,000–€25,000 for reputable licensed counsel; do not attempt these applications without professional help
- **Property transaction costs (if applicable):** Transfer taxes, notary fees, agent commissions — typically 5–10% of property value in most European jurisdictions
- **Annual maintenance costs:** Property management, taxes, insurance, and fund administration fees where applicable
- **Foreign bank account setup:** Most programmes require a local bank account, which takes time and involves compliance documentation
- **Currency conversion costs:** Moving large USD or other currency amounts into EUR involves FX spreads; use a specialist FX provider or negotiate bank rates
- **US tax reporting compliance:** FBAR, FATCA Form 8938, and potentially Form 5471 if you hold interests in foreign entities — a CPA is essential

## 5 Common Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

1. **Treating the headline investment figure as the total budget.**
   *Solution:* Build a comprehensive cost model before committing. Add 10–15% on top of the property purchase price for transaction costs alone, plus all government and legal fees. For a worked model template, see our [How to Compare Residency Routes by Cost and Timeline](/how-to-compare-residency-routes-cost-timeline/) guide.

2. **Assuming a programme will remain open until you are ready.**
   *Solution:* The speed of closures — Portugal's property route, Spain's announced wind-down — demonstrates that golden visa programmes can change or close with limited warning. If you have identified a programme that meets your objectives, engage an attorney and begin due diligence promptly rather than delaying for a "better time."

3. **Using an unqualified or uncertified intermediary.**
   *Solution:* A wave of unregulated "golden visa consultants" operates in this space. Always verify that your immigration advice comes from a licensed attorney in the destination country (and a licensed US attorney or qualified adviser for your US-side compliance). Intermediary fraud and misrepresentation have caused applicants to lose money and receive rejections.

4. **Ignoring the US tax reporting consequences.**
   *Solution:* Foreign real estate held directly does not itself trigger FBAR, but the foreign bank account used to purchase it typically does. Foreign investment fund units may generate PFIC (passive foreign investment company) tax complications under US rules. Before transferring funds abroad, brief a CPA with international tax experience. This is not optional due diligence — it is essential.

5. **Conflating residency and citizenship timelines.**
   *Solution:* A golden visa grants residency, not citizenship. The gap between the two can be substantial — five to ten years of genuine residence, language testing, and renunciation of other citizenships in some cases. If citizenship is your ultimate goal, model the full pathway from day one, not just the first permit stage.

## How US-Based Foreign Nationals Should Think About This

For foreign nationals living in the United States — whether on employment visas, through green cards, or as naturalised citizens — a European golden visa serves a fundamentally different purpose than it does for someone based outside the US.

If you are on a non-immigrant visa with uncertain long-term prospects in the United States, European residency provides a meaningful safety net: a jurisdiction where you have the legal right to live and work, Schengen travel access, and potentially a pathway to EU citizenship over time. It does not require you to leave the US immediately.

If you are a green card holder or US citizen, the calculation shifts. Green card holders who spend significant time abroad risk abandonment of their US permanent residency; consult an immigration attorney before making any extended relocation. US citizens face worldwide taxation regardless of where they reside, which makes the tax environment of the golden visa country highly relevant.

For skilled professionals whose immigration pathway runs through employment sponsorship rather than investment — in Germany, the Netherlands, or elsewhere — the golden visa framework is a parallel track, not a replacement. Employer-sponsored routes described in our guides to [Germany Permanent Residency for Skilled Workers 2026](/germany-permanent-residency-requirements-skilled-workers/) and [Netherlands Work Visa Sponsorship: Trades & Construction](/netherlands-work-visa-sponsorship-trades-construction/) may be more appropriate, more affordable, and faster for those with qualifying employment.

## Due Diligence Checklist Before Applying

Before engaging an attorney and committing investment capital, work through these questions:

- **Is the programme currently accepting new applications?** Verify with an official government source or licensed attorney — not a marketing website.
- **What is the total all-in cost, including taxes, fees, and ongoing maintenance?**
- **What are the physical presence requirements, both to hold the permit and to reach permanent residency/citizenship?**
- **Is the qualifying investment liquid or illiquid, and what is the minimum holding period?**
- **What is the tax treatment of the investment income in the destination country?**
- **What US tax reporting obligations will this investment trigger?**
- **What happens to my permit if the qualifying investment declines in value or the fund/property is sold?**
- **Are there any restrictions tied to my current immigration status in the US or elsewhere?**

## Selecting and Working With an Immigration Attorney

The golden visa space attracts a high volume of marketing from intermediaries, property developers, and fund managers with financial incentives to recommend specific investment products. This is an inherent conflict of interest: the firm selling you a qualifying property also earns commission on the sale.

Best practice is to engage a licensed immigration attorney in the destination country **independently** of any property or fund introduction. The attorney's duty of care runs to you, not to the product seller. Expect to pay for this advice — firms offering "free" immigration guidance are typically subsidising that cost through the investment product they are selling you.

For complex cross-border situations — US tax compliance, existing visa conditions, treaty-country considerations — you may need a team: an immigration attorney in the destination country, a US-qualified attorney or adviser for the domestic compliance side, and a CPA with international tax expertise. This sounds expensive, and it is — but the cost of professional advice is modest relative to the investment minimums involved and the cost of an error.

## The Outlook for European Golden Visas Beyond 2026

EU-level pressure on member state golden visa programmes has been building since the European Commission published its 2022 report criticising the programmes as potential vectors for money laundering and corruption. Several member states have already responded by tightening due diligence requirements, raising minimum thresholds, or closing programmes entirely.

The direction of travel suggests that programmes which survive will become stricter, more expensive, and more genuinely residence-oriented over time. The era of "pay a minimum and hold a Schengen residency card without ever visiting" may narrow considerably over the next five to ten years.

For investors who are serious about European residency through investment, the practical implication is straightforward: the window of relatively accessible programmes is more constrained in 2026 than it was in 2020, and may be narrower still by 2030. That is not a prediction — it is an observation of the regulatory trend that informs the urgency of acting on well-researched plans.
