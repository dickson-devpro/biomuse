The hardest part of moving abroad for work is not the visa. It is finding the small subset of employers who are both licensed to sponsor and willing to do it for the role you want.

Those are two separate things, and most job seekers conflate them. An employer can hold a sponsor licence and still refuse to sponsor you for a particular job, because the salary does not clear the threshold or because they would rather hire someone who needs no paperwork. And an employer can be enthusiastic about hiring you and still be unable to, because they have no licence and are not going to obtain one for a single hire.

This guide is about separating those cases quickly, so you stop spending applications on employers who were never an option.

## Check the register first

The UK Home Office publishes a register of licensed sponsors. It is a public document, updated regularly, and it lists every organisation permitted to sponsor workers along with the routes they are licensed for and their rating.

Before you apply to any UK job that mentions sponsorship, search that register for the employer's name. This takes under a minute and it is the single highest-value habit in an international job search.

Three things to notice when you find a company on it:

**The exact legal name.** Companies trade under names that differ from their registered entity. Search variations before concluding an employer is absent.

**The routes listed.** A licence for one route does not cover another. An organisation licensed only for a temporary worker route cannot sponsor you as a skilled worker.

**The rating.** Sponsors hold a rating, and a downgraded sponsor has restrictions on issuing new certificates. A company on an action plan may not be able to hire you even if it wants to.

If the employer is not on the register, the job is not available to you unless they are willing to apply for a licence, which is a months-long process with fees attached. Occasionally a company will do that for an exceptional candidate. Usually they will not.

Australia maintains comparable information about approved sponsors, and the United States publishes disclosure data on employers who have filed labour condition applications, which is a reasonable proxy for who sponsors and at what salaries. The principle is the same everywhere: there is public data about who actually sponsors, and it is more reliable than a job advert.

## What job adverts are really saying

Learn the vocabulary, because the wording is usually precise and the precision is deliberate.

**"Visa sponsorship available."** Good sign. Usually means the employer is licensed and has budgeted for it.

**"We are a licensed sponsor."** Better. They are telling you to stop worrying about the threshold question.

**"Sponsorship considered for exceptional candidates."** Means they are licensed but will only use it where the alternative is losing someone they really want. Apply if you are genuinely strong for the role; do not apply speculatively.

**"Applicants must have the right to work in the UK."** Means no sponsorship. This is not negotiable and it is not an invitation to explain your situation.

**"We are unable to provide sponsorship at this time."** Means no, and the "at this time" is politeness rather than a hint.

**Silence.** Means almost certainly no. Employers who sponsor usually say so, because it widens their applicant pool and they know it.

## Where sponsorship actually exists

Sponsorship is not evenly spread. Some sectors use it heavily and others almost never do, and knowing which is which saves enormous effort.

**Sectors where sponsorship is routine:** healthcare and social care, higher education and research, specialist engineering, technology in large firms, finance, and professional services firms with international client bases.

**Sectors where it is rare:** retail, hospitality, small professional practices, local government, charities outside specific fields, and almost any business with fewer than fifty employees that has never hired internationally.

**The size effect.** Large organisations sponsor more, not because they are more welcoming but because they have the HR infrastructure, have already paid for the licence, and hire enough people that the fixed costs amortise. A two-hundred-person company that sponsors once a year has a process. A ten-person company does not, and building one for you is an unreasonable ask.

**Public sector healthcare** is in a category of its own in several countries, recruiting internationally at scale with established processes and sometimes with the costs covered.

## Reading salary against the threshold

An employer can be licensed, willing, and still unable to sponsor you — because the salary does not clear the bar.

In the UK, a sponsored skilled worker must be paid the higher of the general threshold, currently **£41,700**, and the published going rate for the occupation code. A lower threshold of **£33,400** applies in some cases outside healthcare and education.

So when you see a sponsored-eligible job advertised at £35,000, one of three things is true: the role sits in a category eligible for the lower threshold, the listing is wrong, or the employer has not thought it through. It is worth asking which, politely, early in the conversation.

In Australia the equivalent figure is the Core Skills Income Threshold, **AUD 79,423** from 1 July 2026, alongside the market rate for the role.

Doing this arithmetic yourself before applying filters out a large share of dead ends.

## A search strategy that works

**Start from the register, not the job board.** Pull the list of licensed sponsors in your sector and region. That is your target list. Then go and look at what those specific companies are hiring for, on their own careers pages, where many roles never reach the aggregators.

**Use the occupation code.** Identify the code that fits your work and look up the going rate. You now know the minimum salary any sponsored offer must carry, which tells you which seniority of role to target. Applying for junior positions is often pointless, because they cannot pay enough to be sponsorable.

**Apply directly.** Agency listings are frequently reposted, out of date, or speculative. A company careers page is current by definition.

**Say it early and plainly.** "I would need sponsorship under the Skilled Worker route" in your first message saves everyone time. Employers who cannot sponsor will tell you immediately, which is a good outcome — you have spent one sentence instead of three interviews.

**Target international graduates' territory.** If you are already in the country on a study visa, you are substantially cheaper to hire, because the study-to-work switch exempts the employer from the Immigration Skills Charge. Say so. It is a real commercial argument and most candidates never make it.

## The scams, which are numerous

Any job search involving immigration attracts predators. The pattern is consistent.

**You are asked to pay.** For the certificate, for the licence, for a "sponsorship fee", for processing. In the UK, charges connected with sponsorship are employer costs and may not be recovered from the worker. Any request for money from an employer or agent for sponsorship itself is either illegal or fraudulent.

**The offer arrives without an interview.** Legitimate employers interview. An offer from a company you never spoke to is not an offer.

**The communication is off.** Free email domains instead of a company domain, a company name that does not match the register, pressure to decide within hours, requests for passport scans before any formal process.

**The role is vague.** Real job descriptions describe work. Fraudulent ones describe salaries and benefits.

Check the employer against the official register. Check the company exists at the address given. Check the email domain matches the website. If an agent is involved, check whether they are registered to give immigration advice where that is regulated.

## Approaching an employer who is not licensed

Occasionally the right job is at a company with no licence. It is not hopeless, but you need to understand what you are asking for.

You are asking a business to apply to the government for a licence, pay a fee, nominate people within the organisation to hold compliance responsibilities, accept ongoing record-keeping duties, and expose itself to audit — all so it can hire one person. Framed that way, the reasons most companies decline become obvious.

It happens when three conditions hold: the role is genuinely hard to fill locally, you are clearly the right person, and someone senior enough to authorise it is personally invested in hiring you. If any of those is missing, it will not happen.

If you do make the case, make it concrete. Tell them the licence exists and is applied for online. Tell them roughly what the compliance obligations are, so the unknown is smaller. Point out that once licensed they can hire internationally again, which turns a one-off cost into an capability. And be honest about the timeline, because a company that applies for a licence expecting you to start next month will be unhappy.

## What happens between offer and start date

Candidates frequently accept an offer and then discover the process has four more steps. The sequence in the UK runs roughly like this.

**The employer assigns a Certificate of Sponsorship.** This is a database entry, not a document. It carries a reference number, the job details, the salary and the occupation code. Check every field when you receive the number, because an error here propagates into your visa application and is tedious to fix afterwards.

**You apply online and pay.** Visa fee and Immigration Health Surcharge together, up front, for the full length of the visa and for each dependant.

**You attend a biometrics appointment.** Fingerprints and photograph, at a visa application centre. In some cases this can be done through an app.

**You submit supporting documents.** Passport, maintenance evidence if required, English evidence if required, qualifications if the role requires them, TB test results if you are applying from a listed country.

**You wait for a decision**, then travel.

The gap between accepting an offer and being able to start is therefore measured in weeks at best, and employers who have not done this before routinely underestimate it. Raise the realistic start date in the offer conversation rather than after.

## Questions worth asking before you accept

Four questions, asked in writing, that save people from the worst outcomes.

**What occupation code will the Certificate of Sponsorship use, and what is its going rate?** This tells you whether the offer is actually sponsorable.

**Will the company cover the visa fee and health surcharge, including for dependants?** The answer is frequently negotiable and almost never volunteered.

**What happens to my status if the role is made redundant in the first year?** Sponsored permission does not survive the end of the sponsorship indefinitely. There is a window to find a new sponsor and it is short. Knowing the number changes how much risk you are taking.

**Who handles the sponsorship internally?** A company with a named person who has done this before is a different proposition from one where it will be figured out by someone in HR between other tasks.

## Adapting your application for a foreign market

Applications are read by people with local conventions, and small mismatches read as carelessness.

**CV conventions differ sharply.** The United States expects no photograph, no date of birth, no marital status, and generally one or two pages. The UK is similar. Much of continental Europe expects a photograph and more personal detail. Australia tolerates longer documents with more detail on each role. Sending a CV in the wrong convention signals that you have not oriented yourself.

**Translate your qualifications.** A degree classification or grading system that means something at home means nothing abroad. Give the equivalent, or describe the standard achieved in plain terms.

**Translate your employers.** A company that everyone in your country knows is anonymous elsewhere. One line of context — the sector, the size, what it does — makes your experience legible.

**Give results, not responsibilities.** This is good advice everywhere and it matters more when the reader cannot calibrate your previous employer's reputation.

**Address the obvious question early.** A line in the covering message saying which route you would need, and that you understand the employer must be a licensed sponsor, demonstrates that you have done your homework and removes the main reason an international application gets set aside.

## Managing expectations honestly

International job searches take longer than domestic ones, usually much longer. You are competing against candidates who require no paperwork, no fees and no waiting period. The employer has to want you enough to accept all three.

That means the realistic strategy is not volume. It is specificity: a smaller number of applications to licensed sponsors in sectors that sponsor, for roles at a seniority that can clear the salary threshold, with a direct and early statement of your position.

Fifty well-targeted applications will do more than five hundred untargeted ones, and will cost you far less of the thing you actually cannot replace, which is time.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I know if a company can sponsor me?**
Search the public register of licensed sponsors for the UK, and the equivalent published data for other countries.

**Can a company sponsor me if it is not on the register?**
Not immediately. It would have to apply for a licence, which takes time and money. Most will not do this for one hire.

**Should I mention I need sponsorship in my application?**
Yes, early and plainly. It saves you from employers who were never able to proceed.

**Can I be asked to pay for my sponsorship?**
No. Sponsorship charges are employer costs. A request for payment is a warning sign.

**Do small companies ever sponsor?**
Occasionally, usually for a role they genuinely cannot fill locally. It is rare enough that you should not build a strategy on it.

**Is it easier if I am already in the country on a student visa?**
Yes, meaningfully. The employer is exempt from the skills charge on a study-to-work switch, and you are available to start immediately.

**What is a Certificate of Sponsorship?**
Not a certificate in any physical sense. It is an entry on a government database with a reference number, created by your employer, recording the job, the salary and the occupation code. You use the number in your visa application.

**How long is a sponsored visa valid?**
Up to five years, depending on what the employer assigns and what you apply for. The fee differs between visas of up to three years and visas of more than three, so the length is also a cost decision for whoever is paying.

**What happens if I lose my sponsored job?**
Your permission is curtailed and you have a limited window to find a new licensed sponsor, switch to another route, or leave. The window is short. Ask about this before you accept, not after.

**Can I do a second job on a sponsored visa?**
Sometimes, within limits, and the rules are specific about hours and the type of work. Do not assume. Check the conditions attached to your grant.

**Do recruitment agencies sponsor?**
Some do, where they employ you directly and place you with clients. Many do not and are advertising a client's role. Ask which it is early, because it determines who needs the licence.

**Is a downgraded sponsor a problem for me?**
It can be. A sponsor with a restricted rating may be unable to assign new certificates while it works through an action plan, which can delay or block your application entirely.
