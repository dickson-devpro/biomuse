**Form 5472 is not a tax form in the traditional sense — it collects no revenue and requires no payment. Yet missing it triggers one of the steepest automatic penalties in the US tax code: $25,000 per form, per year, with no income threshold, no de minimis exception, and a statute of limitations that never starts running until it is filed.** Foreign nationals who own a US LLC or corporation are the primary targets, and the vast majority learn about this form for the first time after the IRS has already sent a bill.

## Why This Matters More in 2026

The IRS cross-referencing capability has improved substantially in recent years. Beneficial ownership data now flows from FinCEN's Corporate Transparency Act reporting into IRS systems, foreign financial institution reporting under FATCA has matured, and the agency has explicitly prioritised international information-return compliance in its published enforcement priorities. In short: the information gap that previously allowed many foreign-owned LLCs to fly under the radar has narrowed considerably.

At the same time, 2026 is the first full tax year in which the CTA's beneficial ownership information (BOI) database is operating at scale for most reporting companies. IRS and FinCEN data-sharing under existing interagency agreements means that a foreign national who correctly files BOI but fails to file Form 5472 creates an obvious mismatch — exactly the kind of automated flag that triggers penalty notices without human review.

For foreign nationals navigating US business ownership alongside other financial obligations — from understanding how to move capital efficiently (see our guide to the [Cheapest Ways to Move Money Between Countries 2026](/cheapest-ways-to-move-money-between-countries/)) to understanding broader US business tax obligations (see [Filing US Business Taxes as a Non-Resident Owner 2026](/filing-us-business-taxes-non-resident-owner/)) — Form 5472 is a non-negotiable piece of the compliance picture.

## What Form 5472 Actually Is

Form 5472 is an information return filed with the IRS to disclose transactions between a US reporting corporation (or a foreign-owned single-member LLC treated as a disregarded entity) and its related foreign parties. The IRS uses it to verify that transactions between the US entity and its foreign owners or affiliates are being reported consistently — and, where transfer pricing rules apply, at arm's-length values.

The form itself runs to a single page plus continuation schedules. It asks for:

- Identifying information about the reporting corporation and the related foreign party
- A description and dollar value of each category of reportable transaction
- Attestation that the information is accurate and complete

The legal authority sits in **IRC Section 6038A** (for US corporations) and **IRC Section 6038C** as extended by Treasury Regulation 1.6038A-2 (extended to foreign-owned single-member LLCs by final regulations effective 2017).

### The Disregarded Entity Trap

Before 2017, a single-member LLC owned by a foreign national was a disregarded entity for US tax purposes — it did not exist separately from its owner in the eyes of the IRS, and many advisors (correctly at the time) told clients no US tax return was required if the LLC had no US-source income. The 2017 regulatory change reversed this for foreign-owned single-member LLCs. The entity remains disregarded for income tax purposes, but it is now **treated as a domestic corporation solely for the purposes of Section 6038A reporting**. That means it must file a pro-forma Form 1120 (a corporate return filed with $0 taxable income, used purely as a transmittal document) with Form 5472 attached.

This structural quirk is the single biggest source of confusion. Owners who know they owe no US income tax correctly file nothing — and unknowingly accumulate $25,000 annual penalties.

## Who Must File: The Core Tests

| Test | Details |
|---|---|
| **Entity type** | US corporation OR single-member LLC treated as a disregarded entity |
| **Ownership threshold** | 25% or more owned (directly, indirectly, or constructively) by one foreign person at any point during the tax year |
| **Transaction trigger** | At least one reportable transaction occurred during the year |
| **"Reportable transaction" scope** | Sales, loans, rentals, services, licensing, contributions, distributions, commissions, and "amounts paid or received in connection with the formation, dissolution, acquisition, and disposition of the entity" |
| **Zero-income exemption** | Does NOT exist. No revenue ≠ no filing requirement |
| **Treaty exemption** | Does NOT exist. Income tax treaties do not override information-return obligations |

### What Qualifies as a Reportable Transaction

The definition is intentionally expansive. The following all count:

- **Capital contributions**: A foreign owner wires $10,000 into the LLC's US bank account to fund operations. Reportable.
- **Expense reimbursements**: The foreign owner pays a supplier invoice on behalf of the LLC from their personal account. Reportable.
- **Loans**: The LLC lends money to the foreign owner, or the foreign owner lends to the LLC. Reportable — and also potentially subject to transfer pricing and interest rules.
- **Management fees**: The LLC pays a management fee to a foreign-owned affiliate. Reportable.
- **Distributions**: The LLC distributes profit back to the foreign owner. Reportable.
- **Rent**: The LLC rents property from the foreign owner or a related foreign entity. Reportable.

One common misconception is that "reportable" means taxable. It does not. These disclosures are information items — the IRS wants to see the flows, not necessarily tax them at the Form 5472 level.

## The Penalty Structure: A Closer Look

The penalty is found at **IRC Section 6038A(d)** and is structured in layers:

1. **Base penalty**: $25,000 per Form 5472, per tax year, imposed the moment the form is missing, late, or substantially incomplete.
2. **Continuation penalty**: If the IRS mails a notice demanding the form and it remains unfiled 90 days later, an additional $25,000 is assessed. This continues in $25,000 increments for every subsequent 90-day period.
3. **Unlimited statute of limitations**: While Form 5472 is outstanding, the IRS can audit the entire tax year — not just the 5472 issues — with no time limit.
4. **Criminal referral potential**: Wilful failure to maintain records required under Section 6038A can be referred for criminal prosecution, though this is reserved for egregious cases.

### Illustrative Example: The Accumulating Bill

*The following is illustrative only and does not represent any real case.*

**Setup**: Mei, a Chinese national living in Shanghai, forms a Delaware LLC in 2022 to sell products on a US e-commerce platform. She contributes $15,000 in seed capital in 2022, earns $40,000 in 2023, and takes a $30,000 distribution in 2024. She is unaware of Form 5472. Her US accountant only prepares a Schedule C (incorrect — the LLC should file a pro-forma 1120 + 5472). In late 2025, the IRS sends a notice.

| Year | Forms Missing | Base Penalty | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022 | 1 × Form 5472 | $25,000 | Capital contribution was a reportable transaction |
| 2023 | 1 × Form 5472 | $25,000 | Revenue flows = reportable transactions |
| 2024 | 1 × Form 5472 | $25,000 | Distribution was a reportable transaction |
| **Total base** | | **$75,000** | Before continuation penalties |

If Mei's representative takes four months (approximately 120 days) to respond after the IRS notice, one additional $25,000 continuation penalty applies per outstanding form — potentially adding another $75,000, bringing the total to $150,000 on a business that earned $40,000 gross in its best year.

This is not a hypothetical edge case. Tax practitioners who work with international clients encounter this scenario regularly.

## Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

### 1. Assuming Zero Income Means Zero Filing Obligation

**Mistake**: The LLC has no revenue this year, so the owner files nothing.

**Solution**: Ask one question — did any money or property move between me and the LLC? If yes (including your initial deposit to open the business bank account), you have a reportable transaction and must file. File the pro-forma 1120 + 5472 even for a startup year with no sales.

---

### 2. Filing a Schedule C Instead of a Pro-Forma 1120

**Mistake**: The owner treats the LLC as a simple pass-through and files a personal Schedule C, which is appropriate for a US resident's single-member LLC but is the wrong form for a foreign-owned disregarded entity.

**Solution**: A foreign-owned single-member LLC files a pro-forma Form 1120 with $0 taxable income and attaches Form 5472. The owner separately reports any US-source income on their own return (Form 1040-NR for non-residents or Form 1040 for US residents). Confirm with a CPA familiar with international tax — this is one area where getting the entity classification right from the start matters enormously.

---

### 3. Omitting Non-Cash Transactions

**Mistake**: The owner reports cash flows but forgets that in-kind contributions — such as transferring intellectual property, equipment, or other property into the LLC — are also reportable transactions.

**Solution**: Before completing Part IV of Form 5472 (the reportable transactions section), prepare a complete list of every asset or service that moved between you, your related foreign entities, and the LLC during the year. Include non-cash items at fair market value.

---

### 4. Missing the "Related Party" Definition

**Mistake**: The owner reports transactions with themselves as the direct owner but forgets transactions with a foreign company they also control. If a foreign affiliate invoices the US LLC for services, that too is a reportable transaction between the LLC and a related foreign party.

**Solution**: Map your entire corporate structure before completing Form 5472. Related parties include any person or entity that owns, directly or indirectly, 25% or more of the LLC — or any entity the LLC or its owners control. If you have a complex structure, a transfer pricing specialist may be warranted.

---

### 5. Filing Late and Not Requesting Reasonable Cause Abatement

**Mistake**: Discovering the missed form, filing it quietly, and assuming the IRS will not notice or will automatically waive the penalty.

**Solution**: If you are filing late, accompany the submission with a written reasonable-cause statement explaining why the failure occurred and demonstrating that you acted in good faith and corrected the issue promptly. First-time penalty abatement may also be available in some cases. Do not file late forms without professional guidance — a poorly worded abatement request can be worse than none.

---

### 6. Treating Form 5472 as Optional for Dormant Companies

**Mistake**: The LLC was formed, a bank account was opened, but the business never launched. The owner assumes a dormant company has no filing obligations.

**Solution**: Opening a bank account and depositing funds — even $1,000 of your own money to maintain the account — constitutes a reportable transaction. Dormant does not mean exempt.

---

### 7. Using a Non-Specialist Accountant

**Mistake**: The owner's general US accountant has no international clients and is unaware of the 2017 regulatory change extending Section 6038A to foreign-owned single-member LLCs.

**Solution**: Verify that your tax preparer has demonstrable experience with international information returns (Forms 5471, 5472, 8858, FBAR). Ask directly: "Have you prepared Form 5472 for a foreign-owned single-member LLC before?" If they hesitate, seek a second opinion.

## Form 5472 vs. Other International Information Returns

Foreign owners of US entities often also have obligations under related international information-return rules. Understanding where Form 5472 fits helps prioritise.

| Form | Who Files | Purpose | Penalty for Non-Filing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Form 5472** | Foreign-owned US corp or SMLLC | Disclose related-party transactions | $25,000/form/year |
| **Form 5471** | US persons who own foreign corporations | Disclose ownership of foreign corps | $10,000–$50,000+/form/year |
| **FBAR (FinCEN 114)** | US persons with foreign accounts >$10,000 | Disclose foreign financial accounts | Up to $10,000–$100,000+/year |
| **Form 8938 (FATCA)** | US persons with foreign assets above threshold | Disclose specified foreign financial assets | $10,000–$50,000+ |
| **Form 8858** | US persons owning disregarded foreign entities | Disclose foreign disregarded entities | $10,000+/form/year |

Note that the Form 5472 penalty of $25,000 exceeds the base penalty for most other international information returns, making it disproportionately costly to miss.

## How Capital Flows Into a US LLC Create Exposure

Many foreign nationals fund their US LLC by wiring money from a foreign bank account. This is efficient, but it creates a web of disclosure obligations that extends beyond Form 5472. The wire itself may need to be disclosed as a reportable transaction on the 5472. If the LLC then holds a US bank account, that account may be relevant to FinCEN BOI reporting. If the foreign owner maintains a foreign account with more than $10,000 at any point, FBAR filing applies separately.

## The Reasonable Cause Defence: What It Requires

The IRS will abate a Form 5472 penalty if you can demonstrate:

1. **Reasonable cause**: You had a legitimate reason for not filing — such as relying on a professional advisor who failed to inform you of the obligation, or being unaware of the 2017 regulatory change that extended the requirement to SMLLCs.
2. **Good faith**: You made efforts to meet your obligations and corrected the problem as soon as you became aware.
3. **No wilful neglect**: The failure was not deliberate or due to careless disregard.

A written statement, submitted with the late-filed forms, should explain the timeline of events, document any professional advice received, and demonstrate that the moment you learned of the requirement, you took action. This is not a DIY project — a CPA or tax attorney familiar with international penalty abatement should draft the statement.

## Voluntary Disclosure and Quiet Disclosure: Know the Difference

**Quiet disclosure** — simply filing overdue forms without explanation — carries risk. The IRS may accept the returns, process the penalty notices anyway, and your late submission serves as confirmation of the violation without any accompanying defence.

**Voluntary disclosure** with a reasonable-cause statement is the preferred approach for most straightforward cases. You file the missing forms, pay any tax owed (if applicable), and simultaneously submit a well-reasoned penalty abatement request. This does not guarantee abatement, but it demonstrates good faith and is far better than hoping the IRS won't notice.

For cases involving multiple years, large sums, or potential criminal exposure, consult a tax attorney, not just a CPA. This publication provides practical information — it does not provide legal or tax advice, and a matter involving large accumulated penalties warrants qualified professional counsel.

## Practical Checklist for Foreign-Owned LLC Owners

Use this as a starting point for your annual compliance review — not as a substitute for professional advice:

- [ ] Confirm your LLC is not 25% or more foreign-owned — if it is, Form 5472 applies
- [ ] List every transaction between yourself, related foreign parties, and the LLC during the year
- [ ] Include non-cash items (property transfers, services, IP licensing) at fair market value
- [ ] Prepare a pro-forma Form 1120 with $0 taxable income if the LLC is a disregarded entity
- [ ] Attach completed Form 5472 to the pro-forma 1120
- [ ] File by April 15, or October 15 with an approved extension
- [ ] Confirm FBAR and Form 8938 obligations for any foreign accounts
- [ ] Verify your CPA has international information-return experience

## Key Takeaways

Form 5472 is not a minor administrative nuisance. It is a high-stakes information return with a $25,000 automatic penalty that applies whether your LLC earned $500,000 or $0. The 2017 extension to foreign-owned single-member LLCs created a massive compliance gap that the IRS is actively closing in 2026.

The most expensive mistake is also the most common: assuming that because no income tax is owed, no filing is required. That assumption costs foreign business owners millions of dollars in aggregate each year — dollars spent on penalties rather than growth.

Get a CPA with documented international experience. File on time. If you have missed prior years, act now rather than hoping the IRS won't look — because in 2026, they are looking.
