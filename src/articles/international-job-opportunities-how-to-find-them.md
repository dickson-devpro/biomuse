The way most people look for work abroad is to open a job board, filter by country, and start applying. Six months later they have sent four hundred applications and heard nothing, and they conclude that the market is closed.

It is not closed. It is filtered, and they were on the wrong side of the filter the whole time. Almost every rejection in an international job search happens before a human reads the application, for a reason that could have been known in advance: the employer cannot sponsor, the salary cannot clear the threshold, or the occupation is not on a list it needed to be on.

This guide is about doing the filtering yourself, first, so that the applications you do send are to employers who can actually say yes.

## Start from your occupation, not from a country

The single most useful question is not "where should I move" but "which countries have a route for what I do."

Occupation is the organising principle of every skilled migration system in the world. Countries publish lists. Points systems weight occupations differently. Salary thresholds are set by occupation code. The same person is highly employable in one country and ineligible in another, purely on classification.

So the first hour of work is this: find your occupation on each country's current list, and note the code. Everything else follows from the code — the going rate you have to beat, whether you need a skills assessment, which assessing authority handles you, and whether the occupation is in a priority category.

If your occupation is not on any list anywhere, that is enormously valuable information to have on day one rather than month nine. The response is usually to look at adjacent occupations your experience would also support, or at study routes that convert into work routes.

## The three systems, and what each demands of you

**Employer-led systems** — the United Kingdom being the clearest example. You cannot apply on your own. A licensed employer must offer you a specific job at or above a salary floor. Your project is therefore a job search, and the people who succeed are the ones who target licensed sponsors specifically.

**Points-based systems** — Australia and Canada. You apply on your own merits. Your project is a documentation exercise: skills assessment, language test, evidence of experience, police certificates. The bottleneck is the assessment and the invitation round, not the job market.

**Cap-and-lottery systems** — most visibly the United States' H-1B. You need an employer, and then you need luck. The implication is that you should never build a plan that depends on a single registration cycle.

Working out which system you are dealing with tells you where to spend your effort. Sending CVs into a points-based system does nothing. Gathering documents for an employer-led system does nothing either. People routinely do both, in the wrong direction.

## Where the demand actually is

Rather than country rankings, which change and are mostly marketing, it is more useful to know which categories of work are consistently sponsored across destinations.

**Healthcare.** Nurses, doctors, allied health professionals, care workers in some systems. The most reliably sponsored category in the world, with established international recruitment pipelines and sometimes employer-funded costs. It also has the heaviest registration requirements — your qualification usually has to be recognised by a professional body before you can work, and that process is separate from immigration and often slower.

**Engineering.** Civil, mechanical, electrical, process. Long-standing shortages, usually on occupation lists, usually requiring a skills assessment.

**Technology.** Developers, data, infrastructure, security. Sponsored widely, though salary floors tend to exclude junior roles.

**Skilled trades.** Electricians, welders, plumbers, heavy vehicle mechanics. Underrated by applicants and in genuine demand, particularly in regional areas of Australia and Canada where additional incentives apply.

**Education.** Teachers in shortage subjects — maths, sciences, special education — and higher education academics.

**Seasonal and agricultural work.** Real, legal, and structurally temporary. These routes rarely lead anywhere permanent, which is the point to be clear-eyed about before taking one.

## Doing your own filtering

A workable sequence:

**One.** Identify your occupation code in each target country, and the going rate or threshold attached to it.

**Two.** Compare that threshold with the seniority you can credibly claim. If the floor is above what your experience commands, you have found the real obstacle, and the answer is either more seniority at home or a different country.

**Three.** Check whether you need a skills assessment or professional registration, and start it now. This is the longest pole in the tent for most occupations.

**Four.** For employer-led countries, build a target list from the official sponsor register rather than from job boards.

**Five.** Sit your language test early. In points systems the band you achieve can be worth more than years of experience, and a retake is cheap relative to a lost year.

**Six.** Apply narrowly and specifically, stating your status in the first line.

## The scams, which are industrial

This search attracts fraud at a scale that few other searches do, because the people conducting it are often far away, under pressure, and unfamiliar with how the destination country's systems work.

**Never pay for a job.** Not for a "work permit deposit", not for "visa processing", not for a placement fee from an employer. Across the major destinations, the costs attached to sponsoring a worker are employer costs by law and cannot be recovered from the worker. Any request for payment in exchange for a job offer is fraudulent.

**An offer without an interview is not an offer.** Real employers interview, often several times.

**Check the employer exists, and that it is licensed.** The UK sponsor register is public. Australia publishes information on approved sponsors. The US publishes labour condition application disclosure data. A company that cannot be found in the relevant official source cannot hire you, regardless of how convincing the letterhead is.

**Watch the small signals.** Free email domains. A company name that is almost but not quite a real company. Pressure to decide today. Documents with inconsistent fonts and misaligned government seals. Requests for your passport scan before any formal stage.

**Check the adviser.** In countries where immigration advice is regulated, there is a public register of licensed advisers. An unregulated agent charging for advice is committing an offence in several jurisdictions.

A simple rule covers most cases: money flows from the employer to you, never the reverse.

## Remote work is not immigration

A growing number of people conflate the two. Working remotely for a foreign employer does not give you the right to live in that employer's country, and in most cases it does not give you the right to work from wherever you happen to be either — doing paid work on a tourist visa generally breaches its conditions, even when the employer and the income are elsewhere.

Digital nomad visas exist in a number of countries and are a genuine option, but read them carefully. Most do not lead to permanent residence, most require you to prove income above a threshold, and many have tax consequences that people discover late.

## Making your application legible to a foreign reader

Applications fail on presentation more often than people realise, because a reader who cannot place your experience has no way to evaluate it.

**Match the local CV convention.** The United States and the United Kingdom expect no photograph, no date of birth, no marital status, and a short document. Much of continental Europe expects a photograph and more personal detail. Australia tolerates a longer document with more depth on each role. A CV in the wrong convention reads as someone who has not looked.

**Contextualise your employers.** One clause — the sector, the size, what the business does — turns an unknown company name into evidence.

**Translate your qualifications into something comparable.** Grading systems do not travel. State the equivalent, or describe plainly what standard you reached.

**Lead with outcomes.** What changed because you were there, with numbers where you have them. This matters more abroad because the reader cannot calibrate against your previous employer's reputation.

**State your position in the first line of the covering message.** Which route you would need, and that you understand the employer must be licensed to sponsor. It takes one sentence and it removes the main reason international applications are quietly set aside.

## Interviewing across borders

**Time zones.** Propose times in both time zones explicitly, with the date, because a meeting that crosses midnight lands on a different day for one of you. Confirm in writing.

**Video setup matters more than it should.** A stable connection, decent light and a quiet room do real work when the interviewer has no other signal about how you operate.

**Expect the status question early.** Answer it plainly: which route, what the employer would need to do, and roughly how long it takes. Candidates who are vague about this read as either unprepared or evasive, and both are fatal.

**Ask about the move.** Who pays the visa fee and health charge. What support exists for relocation. What happens if the role ends in the first year. These are reasonable questions and the answers tell you a great deal about how seriously the company takes international hiring.

## Evaluating an offer properly

A salary figure in a foreign currency is close to meaningless on its own. Convert it into the things it has to pay for.

**Tax.** Marginal rates, social security contributions, and whether your home country also taxes you. US citizens in particular are taxed on worldwide income regardless of where they live, and the relief mechanisms require filing, not just existing.

**Housing.** The largest single variable. A salary that is generous in one city is thin in another a hundred miles away.

**Healthcare.** Whether it is public, insurance-based, or employer-provided, and what you pay either way. Factor in any immigration health charge as part of the cost of the job.

**Schooling**, if you have children. International school fees can exceed the salary increase that prompted the move.

**Pension or superannuation.** What the employer contributes, and whether you can take it with you if you leave.

**The cost of the move itself.** Visa fees and health charges for the whole family, shipping, deposits on a rental in a country where you have no credit history, and the flights.

The useful exercise is to build the household's monthly budget in the destination currency and compare it with what you have now. Many offers that look like large increases are small ones, and some are decreases.

## The first ninety days

**Open a bank account immediately**, because almost everything else depends on it. If you are refused at one bank, try another — policies differ widely and the law usually permits more than the first branch tells you.

**Get your tax number.** Without it many countries withhold tax at a penal rate.

**Register with a doctor** before you need one.

**Accept that you have no credit history.** Expect larger deposits for housing and utilities, and start building a record early through a secured card or an equivalent product.

**Keep every immigration document together and backed up.** Entry stamps, grant letters, residence cards. You will be asked for them at moments you did not expect.

**Diarise your expiry dates.** Visa, passport, residence permit, and any follow-up requirement. The most common way people fall out of status is not a dramatic refusal but a date that nobody was watching.

## Being honest about the timeline

An international move takes longer than people plan for, and the parts that take longest are the parts that are nobody's priority but yours: the assessing authority's queue, the police certificate from a country you left a decade ago, the second language test.

A realistic plan starts the slow items first and runs the fast items in parallel. It does not involve resigning from anything until a decision is in hand. And it assumes at least one round of something going wrong, because something usually does.

## What this guide deliberately avoids

Country rankings for ease of immigration, because they are built on assumptions that will not match your occupation. Salary figures by country, because they vary by city and seniority more than by border. And success rates for any route, because no government publishes figures broken down in a way that would tell you anything about your own case.

What holds steady is the method: occupation first, system second, threshold third, targeted applications last.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which country is easiest to get a work visa for?**
There is no general answer. It depends on your occupation, age, language and qualifications. The question to ask is which countries have a route for your occupation.

**Can I get a job abroad without experience?**
Rarely through skilled routes, because salary thresholds effectively exclude junior roles. Study routes that convert to work routes are the usual path for people early in their careers.

**Do I need to speak the local language?**
For English-speaking destinations you will need to evidence English even if it is your first language, through an approved test unless your nationality exempts you. For other destinations, language requirements vary and are often central.

**Should I use a recruitment agency?**
Legitimate agencies are paid by employers, not by candidates. An agency asking you for money is not one.

**Can my family come?**
Usually, as dependants, with fees and often health charges per person. Check whether your route lets a partner work.

**How long should I expect this to take?**
Longer than the published processing time, because the published time covers only the final step. Plan in quarters, not weeks.

**Is a digital nomad visa a route to living somewhere permanently?**
Usually not. Most are temporary, require proof of foreign income above a threshold, and have no pathway to permanent residence. Check the tax consequences before applying.

**Can I work remotely from another country on a tourist visa?**
Generally no. Performing paid work while on a visitor permission breaches its conditions in most countries, even where the employer and the income are elsewhere.

**Will my professional licence transfer?**
Rarely automatically. Regulated professions — medicine, nursing, law, teaching, engineering in some jurisdictions, accountancy — usually require recognition by a local body, and that process is separate from immigration and often slower.

**Should I learn the language before applying?**
If the destination is not English-speaking, almost certainly yes, and earlier than feels necessary. Language requirements are often both an immigration condition and a practical hiring condition.

**Is it worth applying to several countries simultaneously?**
Yes, particularly across points-based systems where the work is documentation you can reuse. Keep your declarations consistent, because authorities increasingly share information.

**What is the single most common mistake?**
Starting the job search before checking whether the occupation is eligible and whether the target employers can sponsor. The filtering has to come first.
