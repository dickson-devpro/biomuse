**Non-residents can absolutely purchase buy to let properties in the United States and the United Kingdom — but the mortgage products available to you look quite different from what a domestic buyer would see, and the mistakes made by unprepared foreign investors can be costly. Read this guide before you approach a lender: knowing the rules upfront can save you tens of thousands of dollars or pounds in wasted deposits, legal fees, and mispriced deals.**

## Why This Matters in 2026

Cross-border real estate investment has accelerated meaningfully over the past few years. Persistent currency differentials, a growing remote-working professional class, and the expansion of short-term rental platforms have all made buy to let property in the US and UK attractive to investors sitting in Europe, Asia, the Middle East, and Latin America.

At the same time, the regulatory environment has tightened. In the UK, the non-resident Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT) surcharge — currently 2% on top of the standard rates — has been in place since 2021 and shows no sign of being removed. In the US, the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act (FIRPTA) withholding rules mean buyers' agents and settlement agents are now more diligent about flagging foreign national status. Anti-money-laundering scrutiny on large international wire transfers has intensified at both US title companies and UK conveyancers.

The mortgage market itself has also shifted. Rising base rates between 2022 and 2024 compressed yields across both markets. By 2026, central bank rates have stabilised but remain materially higher than the near-zero era of 2020–2021, meaning the arithmetic of leveraged property investment is less forgiving. A gross yield of 5% that looked attractive in 2019 is much thinner against a buy to let mortgage rate of 6–7%. Non-residents, who typically borrow on more expensive specialist products, feel this squeeze first.

For those who understand the landscape, however, opportunities remain real. Urban rental demand is strong in major US metros and in London, Manchester, and Birmingham. Foreign investors who structure their purchases correctly — right ownership vehicle, right lender, right currency strategy — can still generate meaningful returns.

If you are also navigating your own housing situation on arrival, our [First-Time Renters Guide for New Arrivals in the US 2026](/first-time-renters-guide-new-arrivals-us/) covers the renter side of the market as a useful parallel read.

## Who Counts as a Non-Resident for Mortgage Purposes?

"Non-resident" means different things to different lenders, and the definition is not identical to your immigration status.

**In the United States:** Lenders generally distinguish between:

- **US citizens and Green Card holders** — treated as domestic borrowers.
- **Non-permanent resident aliens** — holders of work visas (H-1B, L-1, O-1, etc.) with an SSN. Some conventional lenders will serve this group; others will not.
- **Foreign nationals with no US ties** — no SSN, no US employment, offshore income. This is the most restricted category and the focus of most of this article.

**In the United Kingdom:** Lenders look at whether you are:

- **UK resident** — UK address, paying UK income tax, often with UK bank accounts. Standard buy to let lenders available.
- **Expat** — a UK national living abroad. A distinct product category with its own (smaller) lender pool.
- **Non-resident foreign national** — neither a UK citizen nor UK-resident. The most restricted category, typically requiring specialist or private bank lenders.

Your immigration and tax status interacts with these categories in complex ways. This article does not constitute immigration or tax advice — for questions about how your specific visa, residency, or tax treaty status affects your obligations, please consult a licensed immigration attorney and a CPA with cross-border expertise.

## Buy to Let Mortgages in the United States: A Non-Resident Overview

### The DSCR Loan: The Most Accessible Route

The dominant mortgage product for foreign national buy to let investors in the US in 2026 is the **DSCR (Debt-Service Coverage Ratio) loan**. Unlike a conventional mortgage, which qualifies you on your personal income, a DSCR loan qualifies you on the income the property itself generates.

**How the DSCR ratio works:**

> DSCR = Gross Monthly Rent ÷ Total Monthly Mortgage Payment (principal + interest + taxes + insurance)

Most lenders require a DSCR of at least **1.20–1.25x**. Some will go as low as 1.0x (break-even), but at higher rates or with additional reserves required.

**Illustrative example (not a guarantee of any specific outcome):**

> Maria is a Brazilian citizen with no US credit history or SSN. She identifies a two-bedroom condo in Tampa, Florida, listed at $350,000. A local property manager estimates market rent at $2,400/month. Her target lender requires:
> - 35% down payment: $122,500
> - Loan amount: $227,500 at a rate of 7.5% (30-year fixed, illustrative)
> - Monthly payment (P&I): ~$1,591
> - Property taxes + insurance (escrow): ~$550/month
> - Total monthly obligation: ~$2,141
> - DSCR: $2,400 ÷ $2,141 = **1.12x** — below the 1.20x threshold
>
> To make the deal work, Maria increases her down payment to 40% ($140,000), reducing her loan to $210,000. New monthly P&I: ~$1,468. Total obligation: ~$2,018. DSCR: $2,400 ÷ $2,018 = **1.19x** — still slightly below. She negotiates the purchase price to $335,000 with the same 40% down, or finds a property with slightly higher rent. This illustrates how the DSCR constraint shapes deal selection, not just financing choices.

### Foreign National Loan Programs

Some US lenders offer explicit "foreign national" loan programs separate from their standard DSCR products. These typically feature:

- Acceptance of foreign passport as primary ID (no ITIN required by all lenders, though having one helps)
- Higher rates than domestic products (add 1–2 percentage points as a general starting point)
- Larger reserves required: 12–18 months of mortgage payments held in a US or internationally-verified account
- Shorter maximum loan terms on some products (e.g., 5/1 ARMs rather than 30-year fixed)

### Credit History and ITINs

If you have no US credit history, lenders offering foreign national programs will use alternative underwriting — often a combination of foreign credit reports, bank statements, and the property's rental income. However, having an ITIN and beginning to build a US credit profile before you apply will expand your options significantly. Our article on [how to build a credit file from nothing in a new country](/how-to-build-credit-file-from-nothing-new-country/) covers the practical steps, from secured cards to credit-builder loans.

### Down Payment and Reserves

Expect to put down **30–40%** on a US buy to let as a foreign national. Some lenders go to 25% for very strong profiles or in specific states. Reserves — liquid assets you must demonstrate after closing — are typically 12 months of the full payment (PITIA).

These funds need to be documented and often "seasoned" (sitting in your account for 60–90 days before the loan closes). Large recent deposits trigger source-of-funds questions. If you are moving money internationally to fund your purchase, plan early. The [cheapest ways to move money between countries](/cheapest-ways-to-move-money-between-countries/) guide will help you minimise transfer costs on large sums.

## Buy to Let Mortgages in the United Kingdom: A Non-Resident Overview

The UK has a relatively mature market for non-resident landlord mortgages, but it is concentrated among specialist lenders and private banks rather than high-street names.

### Who Lends to Non-Resident Landlords?

In 2026, major high-street banks (Barclays, HSBC, NatWest) largely do not offer standard buy to let mortgages to non-UK residents applying from abroad. The lenders who do include:

- **Specialist buy to let lenders** with dedicated non-resident or expat products (e.g., certain specialist arms of larger groups)
- **Private banks** for high net worth clients — often starting at £1–2 million in investable assets
- **International mortgage brokers** who aggregate products from multiple specialist lenders

### Loan-to-Value and Rates

| Borrower Category | Max LTV (Typical 2026) | Rate Differential vs UK Resident |
|---|---|---|
| UK resident buy to let | 75–80% | Baseline |
| UK expat (abroad) | 70–75% | +0.3–0.8% |
| EU/EEA non-resident | 65–70% | +0.5–1.2% |
| Non-EU foreign national | 60–70% | +0.8–1.5% |
| Non-resident via private bank | Up to 75% (relationship-dependent) | Negotiated |

*These figures are illustrative ranges based on general market observation in 2026 and will vary by lender, property, and applicant profile. Always obtain quotes from multiple sources.*

### The Stamp Duty Surcharge Stack

UK buy to let purchases carry multiple SDLT surcharges that stack on top of each other for non-resident landlords:

1. **Standard SDLT rates** on the purchase price
2. **+3% buy to let/second home surcharge** (for most purchasers)
3. **+2% non-UK resident surcharge**

**Illustrative example:**

> Anand is an Indian national living in Singapore who wants to buy a £300,000 flat in Manchester for rental purposes.
>
> - Standard SDLT on £300,000: approximately £5,000
> - +3% buy to let surcharge on £300,000: £9,000
> - +2% non-resident surcharge on £300,000: £6,000
> - **Total SDLT: approximately £20,000** (vs ~£5,000 for a UK-resident first-time buyer purchasing the same property as a home)
>
> This is illustrative only. SDLT calculations are complex and change; always obtain a formal SDLT calculation from a UK solicitor before exchange.

This upfront tax cost materially affects yield calculations. A non-resident landlord needs either a higher gross yield or a longer hold period to justify the additional friction.

### Income Documentation and the Non-Resident Landlord Scheme

UK lenders will want to see income — either your overseas employment or business income, or the rental income from the property itself (using an interest coverage ratio, the UK equivalent of DSCR). Most UK buy to let lenders use an **interest coverage ratio (ICR)** of 125–145%, meaning rent must cover 125–145% of the interest payment.

You will also need to register with the UK's **Non-Resident Landlord (NRL) Scheme** with HMRC, which determines how UK rental income is taxed. This is a tax matter — consult a UK-qualified accountant before your first rental payment is received.

## 7 Common Mistakes Non-Resident BTL Investors Make — and How to Avoid Them

1. **Assuming high-street lenders will serve them.**
   *Solution:* Go directly to a mortgage broker who specialises in foreign national or non-resident lending. Standard bank branches will often decline or give inaccurate information at initial contact. A specialist broker knows which lenders are actively writing this business in the current quarter.

2. **Not accounting for the full tax cost before purchase.**
   *Solution:* Model the total acquisition cost including all stamp duty / transfer taxes before signing anything. In the UK especially, the surcharge stack dramatically changes the net yield equation.

3. **Moving funds internationally without a paper trail.**
   *Solution:* Maintain clear, documented records of the source of your deposit funds — bank statements going back at least 12 months, provenance of any large gifts or asset sales, and evidence of the international transfer. Lenders and conveyancers are legally required to perform anti-money-laundering checks; anything that looks unusual will delay or kill your transaction.

4. **Ignoring currency risk in yield calculations.**
   *Solution:* If your mortgage is in GBP but you are a USD earner, or vice versa, factor in exchange rate volatility. Explore multi-currency accounts and FX tools — our guide to [multi-currency accounts compared for expats](/multi-currency-accounts-compared-expats/) walks through the practical options for managing currency friction on ongoing rental income and mortgage payments.

5. **Choosing ownership structure without tax advice.**
   *Solution:* Whether to buy in your personal name, a US LLC, or a UK SPV limited company has significant tax implications in both your home country and the investment country. Never choose a structure purely for asset protection reasons without understanding the full cross-border tax picture. A CPA with international real estate experience is essential before you proceed.

6. **Underestimating reserves requirements.**
   *Solution:* Budget for 12–18 months of mortgage payments to be sitting in a verifiable account at close. This is often the biggest surprise for first-time foreign national borrowers. Combine this with a realistic cash-flow model that includes vacancy, maintenance, and property management fees (typically 8–12% of gross rent in the US; 10–15% in the UK for managed lets).

7. **Treating rental yield as passive and hands-off from day one.**
   *Solution:* Even with a property manager, cross-border landlording has administrative demands — utility of legal notices, insurance renewals, annual tax filings in the investment country. Budget time and professional fees accordingly, and if you are also navigating [property insurance for first-time buyers](/property-insurance-first-time-buyers-explained/), understand that non-resident landlord insurance policies have specific requirements around occupancy and management.

## The Role of Property Investment in Residency Strategies

Some investors are drawn to real estate as part of a broader residency or migration strategy. It is important to be clear about what property ownership does and does not give you.

In the **United States**, owning investment property — even a portfolio of properties — does not create any immigration pathway by itself. The EB-5 investor visa requires investment in a new commercial enterprise that creates jobs, not simple property purchase. Questions about how property investment interacts with your immigration status require a licensed immigration attorney.

In **Europe**, the picture is more varied. Several countries have historically offered residency linked to property investment thresholds. A practical but often overlooked prerequisite for non-resident BTL investing is having the right banking setup. Most lenders require that your deposit funds are held in a bank account in your name before closing. Having a US or UK bank account also simplifies rent collection, mortgage debiting, and tax reporting.

## Working With the Right Professionals

Non-resident buy to let investing is not a solo pursuit. The professional team you need typically includes:

- **A specialist mortgage broker** with active relationships with foreign national or non-resident lenders in the target country
- **A real estate attorney** (US) or **solicitor** (UK) experienced in non-resident transactions
- **A CPA or chartered accountant** with cross-border real estate experience — particularly for ownership structure and tax treaty analysis
- **A property manager** local to the investment market — essential if you will not be nearby to manage the property yourself
- **An FX/international payments specialist** for moving large sums across borders efficiently

The cost of this team is real, but the cost of proceeding without them is typically much higher. Factor professional fees into your deal model from day one.

## Final Checklist Before You Apply

Before you approach a lender for a non-resident buy to let mortgage, confirm you have:

- [ ] Identified your borrowing jurisdiction (US, UK, or other) and confirmed lenders are actively writing non-resident BTL loans there
- [ ] An ITIN (US) or UK tax reference if applicable
- [ ] 30–40% of purchase price available as a deposit, fully documented and sourced
- [ ] 12+ months of reserves on top of the deposit
- [ ] A property manager identified and a realistic rent estimate obtained
- [ ] Run the DSCR (US) or ICR (UK) calculation and confirmed the property meets the threshold
- [ ] Obtained a stamp duty / transfer tax estimate from a solicitor
- [ ] Chosen an ownership structure with advice from a cross-border CPA
- [ ] Set up a bank account in the investment country (or confirmed your lender will accept a foreign account for closing)
- [ ] Documented your international funds transfer and source of wealth

Buy to let investing as a non-resident is genuinely achievable in 2026. The barriers are real but navigable for investors who understand the landscape, build the right team, and do the arithmetic honestly before they commit.
