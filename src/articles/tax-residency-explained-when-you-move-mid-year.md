**When you move to or from the United States partway through a calendar year, your tax situation becomes significantly more complex — you may owe tax in two countries simultaneously, under two different sets of rules, for two distinct periods of the same year.** The IRS calls this a "dual-status year," and it is one of the most misunderstood corners of US international tax law. Understanding your residency start or end date, and the obligations that flow from it, is the single most important financial step you can take in a year you relocate.

---

## Why This Matters More in 2026

Cross-border mobility is at a historic high. The number of individuals entering the US on employment-based visas, intra-company transfer authorisations, and entrepreneur pathways has grown steadily, and so has outbound movement: American citizens relocating to Europe, and foreign nationals completing US assignments and returning home. At the same time, global information exchange has matured. The Common Reporting Standard (CRS) now shares account data across more than 100 jurisdictions. The IRS's FATCA infrastructure continues to expand, and enforcement of foreign account reporting penalties has intensified.

What this means practically: the days when a mid-year move could slip through the cracks are over. Foreign banks report US-indicia accounts to the IRS. Employers file W-2s and W-8BEN forms that reflect residency. Payroll systems flag residency changes. If you get your residency start date wrong — even by a few weeks — you could misclassify months of income, fail to report foreign accounts, miss treaty elections, or overpay tax in one country while underpaying in another.

If you also own a US business as a foreign national, your tax obligations layer further. See our companion guide on [Filing US Business Taxes as a Non-Resident Owner 2026](/filing-us-business-taxes-non-resident-owner/) for how ownership structure interacts with your personal residency status.

## The Two Systems: Tax Residency vs. Immigration Status

Before going further, this distinction must be clear: **tax residency and immigration residency are determined by different laws and operate independently.**

You can be:
- A US immigration non-resident (on an H-1B, L-1, O-1, or similar visa) but a **US tax resident** under the Substantial Presence Test.
- A lawful permanent resident (green card holder) who is also treated as a tax resident of another country under a treaty tie-breaker — which creates its own disclosure and filing obligations.
- A person who has surrendered their green card but may still owe an "exit tax" under the expatriation rules if their net worth or average annual tax liability meets certain thresholds.

The US uses two primary tests to determine whether a foreign national is a **resident alien** (taxed on worldwide income, same as a US citizen) or a **non-resident alien** (taxed only on US-source and effectively connected income).

### Test 1: The Green Card Test

If you were issued a lawful permanent resident card (green card) at any point during the tax year, you are automatically a US tax resident for that entire year — or from the date the card was issued if it was granted mid-year. This is a bright-line rule with almost no exceptions outside treaty positions.

### Test 2: The Substantial Presence Test (SPT)

The SPT applies to foreign nationals who do not hold a green card. You meet it if you were physically present in the US for **at least 31 days during the current year**, and **183 days or more using the following weighted formula**:

- All days present in the **current year** × 1
- Days present in **year 1 prior** × 1/3
- Days present in **year 2 prior** × 1/6

The result must equal 183 or more.

**Illustrative Example — SPT Calculation:**

> Priya arrived from India on a work visa. In 2026, she was present 210 days. In 2025, she was present 90 days. In 2024, she was present 60 days.
>
> SPT count = (210 × 1) + (90 × 1/3) + (60 × 1/6)
> = 210 + 30 + 10
> = **250 days → She MEETS the SPT and is a US tax resident for 2026.**

## When Residency Starts: Arriving in the US Mid-Year

If you meet the SPT for the first time in 2026 and were not a US tax resident in 2025, your residency start date is generally **the first day you were physically present in the US in 2026**.

However, there is an important special rule: if you were present for at least one day in the current year AND you met the SPT in the prior year, your residency may be deemed to start from an earlier date. A CPA should model this carefully before you file.

### The First-Year Choice Election

If you do not meet the SPT in the current year but do meet it in the following year, you may be able to elect to be treated as a resident for part of the current year — the "first-year choice." This election requires:

- You were not a US resident in the prior year.
- You were present in the US for at least 31 consecutive days in the current year.
- You were present for at least 75% of the days from the first day of that 31-day period to December 31.

This is an irreversible election and its benefits (or drawbacks) depend heavily on your specific income pattern. **Do not make this election without professional advice.**

---

## When Residency Ends: Leaving the US Mid-Year

If you were a US tax resident and you leave the US permanently mid-year, your residency end date is generally the **last day of physical presence in the US**, provided you establish a closer connection to a foreign country from that point on and do not return as a resident.

Green card holders have different rules: residency does not end when you leave — it ends when the green card is officially abandoned (Form I-407) or revoked. If you leave the US and simply let your green card "expire" without formally abandoning it, you may continue to be treated as a US tax resident for years.

## Dual-Status Returns: How They Work

A dual-status year means you file **one combined return** that covers two different periods under two different rules:

| Period | Status | What's Taxed | Standard Deduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resident period | Resident alien | Worldwide income | **Not available** (dual-status restriction) |
| Non-resident period | Non-resident alien | US-source income only | Not available |
| Full-year resident | Resident alien | Worldwide income | Available ($14,600 in 2025; indexed for 2026) |
| Full-year non-resident | Non-resident alien | US-source income only | Not available |

**Key restrictions in a dual-status year:**
- You **cannot claim the standard deduction** for either period (with a limited exception for residents of India under the India–US treaty).
- You **cannot file jointly** with a spouse unless you make an election to be treated as a full-year resident (which has its own tax cost implications).
- You **cannot claim certain credits** that are available to full-year residents.

### The Joint Return Election

If you are married and your spouse is a non-resident alien, you may jointly elect to treat your spouse as a US resident for the entire year. This allows a joint return and access to the standard deduction, but it means your spouse's worldwide income for the full year becomes subject to US tax. Whether this is advantageous depends entirely on each spouse's income and treaty position.

## The Closer Connection Exception: An Important Escape Valve

If you were present in the US for fewer than 183 days in the current year (even if the weighted SPT formula would otherwise trigger residency), you may be able to **avoid US tax residency entirely** by filing Form 8840 to claim a Closer Connection to a Foreign Country.

To qualify, you must:
1. Have been present fewer than 183 days in the current year.
2. Have maintained a **tax home** in a foreign country for the entire year (or the relevant portion).
3. Have had a **closer connection** to that country — evidenced by where your family lives, where your bank accounts are held, where you vote, where your personal property is located, where you hold a driver's licence, and similar factors.
4. **Not have applied for a green card** or taken other steps to become a permanent resident.

This exception must be claimed on a timely filed return. If you miss the filing deadline without an extension, the IRS can deny the exception.

---

## Treaty Tie-Breakers: When Two Countries Both Claim You

Most US tax treaties include a tie-breaker provision. If both the US and your home country claim you as a tax resident in the same year (which can easily happen in a year of relocation), the treaty's tie-breaker clause uses a hierarchy of factors to assign residency to one country:

1. **Permanent home** — where you have a permanent home available to you.
2. **Centre of vital interests** — closer personal and economic ties.
3. **Habitual abode** — where you normally live.
4. **Nationality.**
5. **Mutual agreement** between tax authorities.

**Claiming a treaty position is not automatic.** You must file **Form 8833** (Treaty-Based Return Position Disclosure) with your US return, or face a penalty. The treaty position also doesn't exempt you from US filing obligations — it changes what gets taxed, not whether you file.

## Foreign Account Reporting Obligations That Begin Mid-Year

Once you become a US tax resident — even mid-year — your foreign account reporting obligations begin **from your residency start date**, not from January 1.

**FBAR (FinCEN 114):** If the aggregate value of your foreign financial accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point during the portion of the year you were a US resident, you must file an FBAR by April 15 (extendable to October 15). The penalty for wilful failure can be the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance per violation.

**FATCA / Form 8938:** Higher thresholds apply ($50,000 for single filers living in the US; $200,000 for those abroad), but this form is attached directly to your tax return and covers a broader range of foreign financial assets than the FBAR.

If you are moving money between countries as part of your relocation, understand both the tax implications and the transfer costs. Our guide on the [Cheapest Ways to Move Money Between Countries 2026](/cheapest-ways-to-move-money-between-countries/) covers the mechanics of international transfers, but remember that large inbound transfers from foreign accounts may trigger questions from both your bank and, in some cases, the IRS if the source is not documented.

Similarly, if you are investing in US markets as a new resident or still hold foreign brokerage accounts, the classification of those assets changes once you become a US tax resident. Our [Best Brokerage Accounts for Non-Resident Aliens 2026](/best-brokerage-accounts-non-resident-aliens/) guide is relevant if you are in your non-resident period, but note that the applicable account types and withholding rates change once residency begins.

## Worked Illustrative Examples

### Example 1: H-1B Employee Arriving April 1

> **Scenario:** Kwame, a citizen of Ghana, arrives in the US on an H-1B visa on April 1, 2026. He had no US presence in 2024 or 2025. He earns $120,000 from his US employer for the period April 1 – December 31 (9 months), and $18,000 from a consulting contract in Ghana between January 1 and March 31.
>
> **SPT check:** Kwame was present ~275 days in 2026, zero in prior years. 275 × 1 = 275. He meets the SPT. His residency start date is April 1, 2026.
>
> **Filing:** Dual-status return. Resident period = April 1 – December 31. Non-resident period = January 1 – March 31.
>
> - Resident period: $120,000 US salary is fully taxable. Any foreign income earned April 1 onward would also be taxable.
> - Non-resident period: The $18,000 Ghanaian consulting income is **not US-source income** and is not subject to US tax.
> - Standard deduction: **Not available** in a dual-status year.
> - Foreign accounts: If Kwame's Ghanaian bank account exceeded $10,000 at any point after April 1, he must file an FBAR.
>
> *This example is illustrative only. Actual tax due depends on deductions, credits, treaty positions, and other factors. Consult a CPA.*

---

### Example 2: L-1 Executive Departing June 30

> **Scenario:** Mei, a Chinese national who has been a US tax resident since 2022 on an L-1 visa, permanently departs the US on June 30, 2026. She re-establishes her tax home in Singapore immediately. She earned $180,000 from her US employer January 1 – June 30, and $90,000 from her Singapore employer July 1 – December 31.
>
> **Residency end date:** June 30, 2026, assuming she establishes a closer connection to Singapore and does not return as a resident.
>
> **Filing:** Dual-status return. Resident period = January 1 – June 30. Non-resident period = July 1 – December 31.
>
> - Resident period: $180,000 US salary + any worldwide income during that period fully taxable.
> - Non-resident period: The $90,000 Singapore salary is **foreign-source income** and not US-taxable. If she had US-source income during this period (e.g., rental income from a US property), it would still be taxable.
> - Singapore tax: Singapore taxes residents on Singapore-source income. Mei may owe Singapore tax from July 1 onward.
> - The US–Singapore treaty may affect how dividends, interest, and other income items are taxed in each country.
>
> *This example is illustrative only. Consult both a US CPA and a Singapore tax adviser for actual guidance.*

## 7 Common Mistakes — and How to Avoid Them

1. **Assuming your visa start date equals your residency start date.**
   Your tax residency start date is your **first day of physical presence in the US**, not the date your visa was issued or approved. If you entered the US before your employment began — even to find housing — those days count.

   *Solution:* Keep a precise travel log with entry and exit dates, passport stamps, and boarding passes from day one.

2. **Forgetting that exempt-status visa holders are excluded from the SPT — but only for a limited time.**
   F, J, Q, and M visa holders are typically "exempt individuals" who do not count their days toward the SPT. But F-1 students are only exempt for five calendar years total; J-1 non-students for only two years in any six-year period.

   *Solution:* Know your visa category, track your exempt years, and model the SPT from the year your exemption expires.

3. **Claiming the standard deduction on a dual-status return.**
   Taxpayers who complete their own returns using consumer software often see the standard deduction pre-populated. In a dual-status year, **you cannot take the standard deduction** (except in specific treaty situations). Taking it incorrectly underreports your tax.

   *Solution:* Do not use consumer software for a dual-status return. Use a CPA with international tax experience.

4. **Failing to file FBAR and Form 8938 from the residency start date.**
   Many new arrivals assume foreign account reporting begins at the start of the next full tax year. It does not. If your foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point during your resident period, you had an FBAR obligation in your first year.

   *Solution:* As soon as you determine your residency start date, conduct an FBAR analysis covering the remainder of that calendar year.

5. **Abandoning a green card without formal I-407 submission.**
   Simply moving abroad and not renewing your green card does **not** end your US tax residency. The IRS can treat you as a US resident for as long as you hold the card.

   *Solution:* If you intend to leave the US permanently and surrender green card status, file Form I-407 with USCIS and document the date of abandonment. This is an immigration action — consult a licensed immigration attorney.

6. **Overlooking the "exit tax" for long-term residents.**
   If you have held a green card for eight or more of the last fifteen years and you relinquish it, you may be subject to the expatriation tax rules under IRC §877A. These rules can treat you as having sold all your worldwide assets on the day before your departure. The thresholds (net worth above $2 million, or average annual net tax liability above a threshold indexed annually) catch more people than they expect.

   *Solution:* Model exit tax exposure **before** you formally abandon your green card. A CPA specialising in expatriation planning is essential here.

7. **Mishandling income from foreign rental property acquired before arrival.**
   If you own rental property abroad and you become a US tax resident, rental income from that property is now subject to US tax during your resident period. Many new arrivals are unaware their foreign property income must be reported.

   *Solution:* Compile records of all foreign rental income and expenses. Foreign tax credits may reduce double taxation, but you must claim them correctly. If you are considering purchasing property abroad as a non-resident, see our [Buy to Let Mortgages for Non-Residents: 2026 Guide](/buy-to-let-mortgages-non-residents/) for structural considerations that interact with residency status.

## How State Tax Residency Works Separately

Everything above addresses **federal** tax residency. Each US state has its own rules. Some states (like California and New York) are aggressive about asserting residency and can claim you as a state tax resident even after you believe you have left — particularly if you maintain property, a driver's licence, family, or business ties there.

State "domicile" is a separate legal concept from federal tax residency. Changing your federal residency status does not automatically change your state domicile. If you are relocating within the US or departing from a high-tax state, document the change of domicile carefully: update voter registration, driver's licence, vehicle registration, and bank account addresses; resign from local boards or clubs; establish new ties in your new jurisdiction.

## Practical Filing Checklist for a Mid-Year Move

Use this as a starting framework. Your actual obligations will differ based on your specific facts.

**Before you file:**
- [ ] Determine exact US arrival or departure date and build your travel log.
- [ ] Calculate the SPT weighted count for the current year and prior two years.
- [ ] Identify your residency start or end date.
- [ ] Check whether any treaty tie-breaker applies (Form 8833 may be required).
- [ ] Compile all income from both periods: US-source and worldwide.
- [ ] Identify all foreign financial accounts and maximum balances during the resident period.
- [ ] Assess whether FBAR and/or Form 8938 thresholds were met.
- [ ] Determine if you owned a foreign business — PFIC, CFC, or foreign partnership reporting may apply.
- [ ] Engage a CPA with international/expatriate tax experience.

**At filing:**
- [ ] File the appropriate dual-status return (Form 1040 as the primary, with Form 1040-NR as an attachment for the non-resident period, or vice versa depending on direction of move).
- [ ] File FBAR via FinCEN's BSA E-Filing System by April 15 (October 15 with automatic extension).
- [ ] Attach Form 8938 to your tax return if FATCA thresholds are met.
- [ ] Attach Form 8833 if claiming a treaty position.
- [ ] File applicable state returns.

If your business has foreign owners or you are a foreign national running a US LLC, the reporting requirements compound significantly. Our article on [Form 5472 and the Penalty Most Foreign Owners Miss](/form-5472-penalty-foreign-owned-llc/) details one of the most commonly overlooked corporate filing obligations — with penalties starting at $25,000 per form.

## When to Get Professional Help

This article provides a framework for understanding how mid-year tax residency works. It is **not tax advice**, and the rules described here interact in ways that are highly sensitive to individual facts: the number of days present, the type of visa, treaty eligibility, the nature of income, state of domicile, asset holdings, and more.

The cost of hiring a qualified international tax CPA for a dual-status year is typically several hundred to several thousand dollars depending on complexity. That cost is almost always justified given the penalties for FBAR violations alone — to say nothing of the risk of paying tax in the wrong country, or missing elections that could save you materially.

If you are also navigating the residency implications in your home country — for example, whether leaving triggers a deemed disposal of assets, a departure tax, or a change in pension entitlements — you may need advisers in both jurisdictions working in coordination.

## The Bottom Line

Tax residency in a year you move is not a simple question, and the IRS does not extend much mercy for errors made in good faith by people who did not know the rules. Your residency start or end date drives everything: which income is taxable by the US, whether you can claim deductions, what foreign account forms you must file, and whether a treaty can provide relief.

The most important actions you can take are: keep a meticulous travel log, determine your residency dates early, identify your foreign account reporting obligations from day one, and work with a CPA who specialises in international individual taxation. Getting this right in the first year you move sets the foundation for compliant, efficient filing in every subsequent year.
